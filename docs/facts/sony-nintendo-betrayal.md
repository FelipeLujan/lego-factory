## Brief
- **Category:** History story (a corporate double-cross, 1991 Consumer Electronics Show, Chicago).
- **Hook idea (spoken line 1, 10 words):** "Nintendo dumped Sony in public, and created its biggest rival."
  - Hook title (works on mute; adds to the line instead of repeating it): **"ONE DAY LATER: PHILIPS."** (or **"24 HOURS FROM PARTNER TO RIVAL"**; "the next day" is verified, "24 hours" is only approximate, so the first one is safer.)
  - Frame-0 visual: a brick trade-show stage. A banner reading "PLAY STATION" (two words, generic font, no logos) lights up as a spotlight swings away to a second stage across the hall, where a new handshake is happening.
  - Wording note: say "in public" or "at the same trade show", not "on Sony's stage". Nintendo announced at its own press event at the same show.
- **Story arc (about 66 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, hook:** the two stages and the spotlight swinging away.
  2. **3–12 s, setup:** in 1991 Nintendo controlled about 80% of the U.S. video game business. Its power came from controlling the games: every cartridge went through Nintendo. Sony, a giant in electronics, makes a deal to build a CD-based machine with Nintendo.
  3. **12–22 s, the fine print:** under that deal Sony keeps licensing control over every CD game for its machine, and Sony plans games from its own movie and music companies. An analyst at the time: "I don't think they realized Sony would be so aggressive on software."
  4. **22–32 s, Friday:** at the Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago, Sony proudly announces the "Play Station", a machine that plays Nintendo cartridges and CDs.
  5. **32–42 s, "Move one" (Saturday):** the next day Nintendo announces it is working with Philips, one of Sony's main rivals, on a different CD system. Sony's executive says he was "very surprised" and that the "spirit of the agreement has been violated". Nintendo's Howard Lincoln says it was for "technical reasons".
  6. **42–52 s, the backfire:** Sony's president Norio Ohga, egged on by engineer Ken Kutaragi, decides that Sony will go it alone. Sony sets up Sony Computer Entertainment in 1993 and launches its own PlayStation in December 1994.
  7. **52–60 s, the payoff:** the original PlayStation ships more than 102 million units. The Philips CD add-on for Nintendo never came out.
  8. **60–66 s, takeaway + soft CTA,** then the 2.4 s end-card hold.
- **Tactics (1–2):**
  1. **Read who holds the control before you sign.** Nintendo's real mistake was signing away licensing control over CD games. An LA Times analysis headline at the time: "Nintendo Made Big Error in Negotiating Game." A deal that leaves one side holding all the control is unstable: the other side will look for a way out.
  2. **A walk-away alternative (BATNA) is leverage, but a public ambush has a price.** Nintendo lined up Philips as its alternative (BATNA, "best alternative to a negotiated agreement", is the term from Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes*, 1981). Using it as a public surprise humiliated its partner and turned that partner into a competitor.
- **Takeaway (one line):** "A deal that gives one side all the control won't stay a deal." (A sharper version: "Fix the control in the contract, or your partner becomes your rival.")

## Facts
**The deal and the stakes**
- Sony and Nintendo had a deal for Sony to market a new CD-based video game system. Sony announced it on the Friday "on the eve of the biannual Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago". [LA Times, Jonathan Weber, "Sony, Nintendo Plan Video Game System", June 1 1991]
- The machine was called the **Play Station**. It would play a new generation of CD games and also Nintendo cartridges for Nintendo's new 16-bit system. Sony planned it for late 1991 in Japan and early 1992 in the U.S. at "several hundred dollars". [LA Times June 1 1991; LA Times, Jonathan Weber, "News Analysis: Nintendo Made Big Error in Negotiating Game", June 4 1991] The NYT described it as a machine with two slots, one for Nintendo's new cartridges and one for CD games. [NYT, Eben Shapiro, "Nintendo-Philips Deal Is a Slap at Sony", June 3 1991]
- Sony planned at least 10 CD games at launch, most of them based on movies from its Columbia Pictures affiliates and music from its record company (formerly CBS Records). [LA Times June 1 1991] The NYT mentioned a planned game based on the upcoming movie *Hook* and a possible game featuring Michael Jackson. [NYT June 3 1991]
- Nintendo controlled "an estimated 80% of the $5 billion U.S. video game business". [LA Times June 1 1991] The NYT called it "the $4.7 billion video game market". [NYT June 3 1991]
- **The control clause:** under the deal, Sony had the right to sell and profit from the game discs, and Nintendo disputed how broad that right was. [NYT June 3 1991] "Sony will retain licensing control over any compact disc games that play on its machine." [LA Times June 4 1991] "Sony asserts that it alone controls the rights to profit from software made for the compact disk component of its Play Station." [NYT June 3 1991]
- Nintendo's strategy had always been tight control over game software. [LA Times June 4 1991] Howard Lincoln: "The key to the video game industry is software, not the hardware." [NYT June 3 1991]
- Industry executives said they were surprised that Nintendo had apparently given up the right to profit from games sold on the Sony CD machines. Some experts called it an unfavorable deal for Nintendo. [NYT June 3 1991] The LA Times analysis said Nintendo "apparently made a rare strategic error" when it agreed to work with Sony. [LA Times June 4 1991]
- Analyst Jonathan Seybold: "I don't think Nintendo anticipated that Sony would compete with them head-to-head... I don't think they realized Sony would be so aggressive on software." [LA Times June 4 1991]
- Later accounts say the Sony deal dated from 1988 and gave Sony control of the "Super Disc" CD format and its licensing. Nintendo president Hiroshi Yamauchi reportedly found that unacceptable. [Wikipedia "Super NES CD-ROM" and "PlayStation (console)", citing Edge "The Making Of: PlayStation" 2009, IGN 1998 and Retro Gamer's *The PlayStation Book* 2015] (Note: the 1991 LA Times analysis says Nintendo agreed "last year". Don't state the year of the contract.)

**The double-cross**
- On Saturday, at the show, Nintendo "created a stir" by announcing it was joining forces with Philips. Philips technology would be used for a CD add-on for Nintendo machines. [NYT June 3 1991] "A day after Sony announced a deal with Nintendo... Nintendo announced that it had struck a deal with Philips, one of Sony's principal rivals." [NYT June 3 1991]
- Philips was one of the inventors of the compact disc and "a fierce rival — and sometime partner — of Sony's". [LA Times June 4 1991] The Sony and Philips disc systems were not compatible. [NYT June 3 1991]
- Under the Philips deal, Nintendo-branded games could also play on Philips' CD-i player, and Philips did not get the right to put a Nintendo cartridge slot in any machine it made. [NYT June 3 1991; LA Times June 4 1991]
- Nintendo had scheduled a press conference to launch its new cartridge system, and its prepared materials did not mention Philips. The LA Times reported that once Sony announced on Friday, "Nintendo was forced to make some kind of response." [LA Times June 4 1991] The Philips news upstaged Nintendo's own long-planned launch of its more powerful system. [NYT June 3 1991]
- Olaf Olafsson, president of Sony Electronic Publishing, said he was "very surprised to hear the Nintendo-Philips announcement" and that the "spirit of the agreement has been violated". [LA Times June 4 1991] He also said: "We view this as a very serious matter." [NYT June 3 1991]
- Howard Lincoln (senior vice president, Nintendo of America) said Nintendo chose Philips for technical reasons: "Our engineers reached the conclusion that from a technical standpoint that it was better for Nintendo to work with Philips." He declined to compare the two deals and confirmed "There is a dispute between Sony and Nintendo as to the terms of the agreement." [NYT June 3 1991]
- Olafsson speculated that the Philips deal might be a negotiating ploy on Nintendo's part and left the door open to revising the deal. [LA Times June 4 1991]
- A later account says Yamauchi sent Nintendo of America's Minoru Arakawa and Howard Lincoln to Europe to negotiate the Philips deal, and that the new contract gave Nintendo control over its licences. [Wikipedia "PlayStation (console)", citing McFerran, *The PlayStation Book* (Retro Gamer, 2015) and IGN 1998] → say "reportedly".
- Retro Gamer's *The PlayStation Book* has Howard Lincoln stepping onto the stage at 9 a.m. the next day and calling it "the greatest ever betrayal" in the industry. [Wikipedia "PlayStation (console)", citing McFerran 2015 p.10] → hedge "reportedly" for the on-stage detail. Journalists commonly call it "the greatest ever betrayal". [Wikipedia "Super NES CD-ROM"]

**The backfire**
- Sony's president Norio Ohga was furious. Egged on by engineer Ken Kutaragi, he decided to create Sony Computer Entertainment, a division headed by Kutaragi that would take Sony into the console business. [Tristan Donovan, *Replay: The History of Video Games*, excerpt in VentureBeat, June 23 2018] → this is the author's account; say "reportedly furious".
- Kutaragi is widely called "the Father of the PlayStation". [Wikipedia "PlayStation (console)", citing multiple outlets]
- Talks between Sony and Nintendo continued for a while after the show, and Sony later abandoned the SNES-based machine for its own standalone console. [Wikipedia "Super NES CD-ROM", citing Asakura, *Revolutionaries at Sony* (McGraw-Hill, 2000); NYT June 3 1991 ("expected to try to settle their differences through negotiation")]
- November 1993: Sony and Sony Music Entertainment jointly founded Sony Computer Entertainment. [SCE corporate history page (archived, scei.co.jp); SIE "Our company" page: "Since launching in 1993 as Sony Computer Entertainment"]
- December 3, 1994: PlayStation (SCPH-1000) launched in Japan at ¥39,800. September 1995: it launched in North America at $299 and in Europe. [SCE business data and corporate history (archived scei.co.jp)]
- The original PlayStation shipped **more than 102.4 million** units worldwide. [Sony Interactive Entertainment, Business Data & Sales page] (SCE's archived table: 102.49 million.) PlayStation 2: more than 160 million. [SIE Business Data & Sales]
- For comparison, the Nintendo 64 sold 32.93 million units. [Nintendo IR historical sales data, as reported by GamesIndustry.biz / Wikipedia] → if used, say "about 33 million" and "roughly three to one".
- The Nintendo–Philips CD add-on for the Super NES was never released. Nintendo reportedly cancelled it quietly by 1993. Philips gained licences to some Nintendo characters for its CD-i games, which were poorly received. Nintendo's first disc-based console came in 2001 (GameCube). [Wikipedia "Super NES CD-ROM", citing Super Play / Retro Gamer / GamePro]
- One of the roughly 200–300 prototypes of the SNES-based Sony "Play Station" surfaced in 2015 and sold at Heritage Auctions in 2020 for $360,000 (a $300,000 bid plus the buyer's premium) to collector Greg McLemore. [Forbes / Kotaku 2020; Wikipedia "Super NES CD-ROM"; Guinness World Records] (Optional kicker; keep "roughly" on the prototype count.)

## Do not say (UNSURE or disputed; left out)
- **Exact dates.** Sources disagree: the 1991 NYT and LA Times say Sony announced on **Friday** (May 31, 1991) and Nintendo on **Saturday** (June 1). Many retellings say June 1 and June 2, and Donovan's *Replay* says May 28. Say "at the 1991 summer Consumer Electronics Show in Chicago" and "the next day". **Not Las Vegas** (one Wikipedia line says Las Vegas; the 1991 show was in Chicago).
- **"The next morning"** as a flat fact. Only Retro Gamer's book gives "9 a.m. the next day". Say "the next day".
- **"Sony stood on stage" / "Nintendo announced on Sony's stage."** Sony's announcement was made "on the eve of" the show (LA Times), and Nintendo announced at its own press event. Show two stages in the same hall, never Nintendo hijacking Sony's stage.
- **"Without any warning" / "Sony found out live in the audience."** Disputed. The 1991 LA Times quotes Sony's Olafsson as "very surprised". But Asakura's *Revolutionaries at Sony* (via Wikipedia) says Kutaragi and Nobuyuki Idei learned of Nintendo's move **two days before** the show. A Seattle Times wire report dated May 31, 1991 (Tokyo) already said Nintendo would put games on Philips CDs, and the June 1 LA Times mentioned a reported Nintendo–Philips CD-i deal. Use: "Sony said it was 'very surprised'." Optional curiosity loop (hedged): "Sony reportedly heard two days earlier, and announced anyway."
- **Calling the Play Station just "a Nintendo CD add-on".** The machine Sony announced in 1991 was a Sony-made machine that played Nintendo cartridges and CDs. Nintendo's own plan was a CD add-on, first with Sony and later with Philips. Say "a machine that played Nintendo games and CDs."
- **"Nintendo cancelled the Sony deal that day / ripped up the contract."** In June 1991 Lincoln confirmed a dispute, Sony could still build in the Nintendo cartridge slot under the signed agreement (LA Times), and talks continued. Say "Nintendo announced it would go with Philips instead."
- **The year of the contract (1988).** The later books say 1988, but the 1991 LA Times says "last year". Leave the year out.
- **Sheff's *Game Over* quote** ("give Nintendo back its stranglehold on software and gracefully f--k Sony"). It's profane and seen only second-hand (via Wikipedia). Leave it out.
- **Ohga's line "If you believe you can do it, then do it!"** Seen only on secondary blogs. Leave it out.
- **The "unwritten law" of Japanese companies / outrage in Japan's business community.** It's in Wikipedia without a source we checked. Leave it out.
- **Sony nearly partnering with Sega** (Tom Kalinske's story). It's real per USgamer 2013 but off-topic. Leave it out.
- **Sony "cut all ties in May 1992"** and **"200 prototypes" without a hedge.** Single-source (Asakura via Wikipedia); hedge or leave out.
- **"Biggest rival" as a sales claim.** It's fine as a characterisation (VentureBeat headline: "its fiercest rival"), but don't claim PlayStation outsold every Nintendo console. Nintendo's DS, Switch and Wii are huge sellers. Compare only PS1 vs N64.
- **No invented dialogue** between Yamauchi, Ohga, Kutaragi or Lincoln beyond the quotes above. **No real logos** (Sony, PlayStation, Nintendo, Philips) in the 3D geometry; names may be spoken or shown in captions.
