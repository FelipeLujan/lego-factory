# because-copier-trick: The "Because" Trick, and the 20-page request where it stopped working

Format: `docs/DEALCALM-TOFU.md` (60–75 s, soft CTA, triple hook, "DEALCALM STORIES" stinger). Category: **tactic explainer** (Langer, Blank & Chanowitz 1978, as retold in Cialdini's *Influence*), plus a short **dramatized everyday scenario**. Research claims were checked with web search on 2026-11-17, and the core numbers were read **directly from the original paper's PDF** (JPSP 36(6):635–642, Table 1 and the Method section). The everyday scenes are fiction: frame them as "Imagine…". They carry no statistics. Saved to `docs/facts/because-copier-trick.md`.

## Brief
- **Category:** Tactic explainer (giving a reason / "placebic" information / mindless compliance) + a dramatized everyday scenario (a small ask vs. a big ask such as a raise).
- **Hook idea (≤ 14 words):** "A nonsense reason got 93% of strangers to let them cut in." (12 words). Alternates: "'Because I have to make copies.' 93% of strangers said yes anyway." (11) / "One word plus a nonsense reason took a yes from 60% to 93%." (13)
  - The requested hook ("One word got 93% of strangers to let them cut the line, even with a nonsense reason") is **17 words**, so it's over the 14-word limit. It also says "the line", but the study approached **one person** already at the copier (sessions skipped any time a line had formed). Say "cut in" / "go first" instead.
  - Frame-0 visual: a minifig is about to drop a coin into a beige brick copier when a second minifig steps in with a 5-sheet stack. A big brick word **"BECAUSE"** slams down between them, and a scoreboard flips **60% → 93%**. Hook title (works on mute, adds rather than repeats): **"THE REASON WAS FAKE."**
- **Story arc (~60–75 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, hook:** coin, copier, "BECAUSE" slam, scoreboard 60 → 93.
  2. **3–14 s, the setup (1978, a university library copier in New York):** a researcher waits at a table. Each time someone puts their pages on the copier, the researcher steps in just before the coin goes in: "Excuse me, I have 5 pages. May I use the xerox machine?" With no reason, **60%** said yes.
  3. **14–24 s, two reasons:** "…because I'm in a rush?" → **94%**. Then the strange one: "…because I have to make copies?" Everyone at a copier has to make copies, so it adds nothing, and still **93%** said yes. Name it: **"a placebo reason"** (the paper calls it *placebic* information). Cialdini's take: the word "because" set off an automatic yes. *Click, whirr.*
  4. **24–30 s, curiosity loop:** "So any reason works?" The brick paper stack grows from 5 sheets to **20**.
  5. **30–42 s, the twist:** asking to copy 20 pages, with no reason: **24%**. With "because I have to make copies": still **24%**, so the fake reason did nothing. Only the real reason, "I'm in a rush", moved it, to **42%**. When the favour cost more, people actually listened to the reason.
  6. **42–56 s, the move (dramatized):** *"Imagine asking a coworker to swap desks for an hour: almost any 'because' will do. Now imagine asking your boss for a raise."* The brick scale tips: "because I'd like more" sits as a light grey tile; "because I took on the second region and here's what it brought in" sits as a heavy gold block. Rule on screen: **small ask → give a reason. Big ask → give a real one.**
  7. **56–64 s, the defence:** when someone hands *you* a "because", check whether there's anything after it. "Because that's our policy." Is that a reason, or just the word?
  8. **64–72 s, takeaway + soft CTA**, then the end card (`DealCalm` / `App Store · dealcalm.com`, 2.4 s hold).
- **Tactics (2):**
  1. **Always attach a reason ("because…"):** for small, routine requests, people tend to say yes to the *shape* of a request with a reason, even an empty one (Langer et al. 1978).
  2. **Raise the stakes, raise the reason:** when the ask costs the other side something real, an empty reason works no better than none (in the 20-page condition, 24% vs 24%). Only a genuine reason helped (42%). The other side of this: when a "because" is aimed at you, check what comes after it.
- **Takeaway (one line):** "Always give a reason. When the stakes are high, make it a real one." (alt: "'Because' opens the door. Only a real reason gets you through it.")
- **Soft CTA line idea (≤ 8 words, spoken "Deal Calm"):** "Practice giving real reasons with Deal Calm." (7 words) / alt: "Rehearse your big ask with Deal Calm."

## Facts
**The study (primary source, read directly)**
- Ellen J. Langer, Arthur Blank and Benzion Chanowitz, "The mindlessness of ostensibly thoughtful action: The role of 'placebic' information in interpersonal interaction," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 1978, vol. 36, no. 6, pp. 635–642. DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.36.6.635. [Crossref record; the paper's PDF]
- Bylines: Langer, **Harvard University**; Blank and Chanowitz, **The Graduate Center, City University of New York**. The paper reports **three field experiments**; the copier study is Experiment 1. [paper, p. 635]
- Experiment 1 took place at **the copying machine at the Graduate Center of the City University of New York** (a library copier). Its **120 adults** (68 men, 52 women) were simply people who came to use the machine. [paper, pp. 636–637]
- The researchers approached **one person at a time**. They skipped anyone if a line had already formed when the experimenter arrived, or if someone else walked up right after a subject was approached, and they waited at least 5 minutes between subjects. [paper, p. 637] (So "cut in front of someone" is accurate; "cut the line" is not quite.)
- The experimenter sat at a library table with a view of the copier. When a person put their pages on the copier, the experimenter approached **just before they put in the money** to start copying, and asked to go first. [paper, p. 637] (A coin-operated copier is accurate set dressing.)
- The three exact requests [paper, p. 637]:
  1. Request only: **"Excuse me, I have 5 (20) pages. May I use the xerox machine?"**
  2. Placebic information: **"Excuse me, I have 5 (20) pages. May I use the xerox machine, because I have to make copies?"**
  3. Real information: **"Excuse me, I have 5 (20) pages. May I use the xerox machine, because I'm in a rush?"**
- Small vs. big favour: the experimenter asked to copy either 5 or 20 pages. The paper counted the favour as "small" if the subject had more pages than the experimenter and "large" if the subject had fewer. [paper, p. 637] (Say "5 pages" vs "20 pages"; that's the shorthand the paper itself uses.)
- **Results (Table 1, share of subjects who agreed)** [paper, p. 637]:
  | | No reason | Placebic ("have to make copies") | Real ("in a rush") |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | Small (5 pages) | **.60** (n = 15) | **.93** (n = 15) | **.94** (n = 16) |
  | Big (20 pages) | **.24** (n = 25) | **.24** (n = 25) | **.42** (n = 24) |
  - The cell sizes add up to 120, and the percentages match whole people: 9/15 = 60%, 14/15 ≈ 93%, 15/16 ≈ 94%, 6/25 = 24%, 10/24 ≈ 42%. (On screen, "about 14 out of 15 people" is a fair way to show 93%.)
- For the small favour, the placebic and real-reason groups were "virtually identical" and **significantly different** from the no-reason group (p < .05). [paper, p. 638]
- For the big favour, the no-reason and placebic groups were "identical" and **"tended to be different"** from the real-reason group, a **marginal** result (reported as .10 > p > .05). [paper, p. 638] (Script: "only the real reason moved the number". Don't say "significantly".)
- The authors' summary: if a reason was given, the subject "was more likely to comply than if no reason was presented, even if the reason conveyed no information". Once compliance required "a modicum of effort", that changed. [paper, p. 638]
- The paper describes the pair as "two people who are strangers to each other". [paper, p. 638] (So "strangers" in the hook is accurate.)
- Half the sessions were run by a **female experimenter who did not know the hypotheses**, half by a **male experimenter who did**. The female experimenter got more compliance overall, but there were no interactions with the other variables, so the table combines them. [paper, p. 637]
- The paper's term for the empty reason is **"placebic" information**: redundant information that has the *structure* of a reason. [paper, title and p. 637]

**Cialdini's *Influence***
- Robert Cialdini retells the copier study in chapter 1 of *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* ("Weapons of Influence"), with the 60% / 94% / 93% figures for the 5-page request. [*Influence*, ch. 1, as quoted by James Clear and other summaries]
- Cialdini's framing: "A well-known principle of human behavior says that when we ask someone to do us a favor we will be more successful if we provide a reason. People simply like to have reasons for what they do." [*Influence*, ch. 1, as quoted at jamesclear.com/copy-machine-study]
- Cialdini ties the result to the word **"because"** as an automatic trigger ("click, whirr"), comparing it to mother turkeys responding to a chick's "cheep-cheep" sound. [*Influence*, ch. 1, via archive.org text and summaries] (Cialdini's edition reportedly words the fake reason "because I have to make some copies". Use the paper's wording, "because I have to make copies".)

**Follow-up and debate**
- Valerie Folkes published "Mindlessness or mindfulness: A partial replication and extension of Langer, Blank, and Chanowitz," *JPSP* 48(3):600–604 (1985). Langer, Chanowitz and Blank answered in the same issue: "Mindlessness–mindfulness in perspective: A reply to Valerie Folkes," pp. 605–607. [Crossref records]
- Folkes reportedly concluded that everyday interactions "do not appear to be as mindless (automatic) as might have been surmised", and that people responded to the *quality* of the reason. [Folkes 1985, as summarized; abstract not read directly] (Optional context only: it actually *supports* the video's lesson that real reasons matter.)

**Dramatized examples (fiction, frame as "Imagine…")**
- The coworker desk swap and the raise request are **illustrative fiction**. They come with no numbers, and nothing should suggest the 1978 percentages apply to raises, salaries or negotiations.

## Do not say
- **"Cut the line" / "people waiting in line."** The study deliberately approached a single person already at the copier and skipped sessions with a line. Say "cut in", "go first" or "jump ahead at the copier".
- **"One word got 93%" on its own.** The 93% came from the word *plus* an empty reason, and 60% already said yes with no reason at all. Say "one word and a nonsense reason took it from 60% to 93%".
- **"The fake reason worked as well as a real one, always."** That held only for the small (5-page) request in that study.
- **"Real reasons significantly beat fake ones for big requests."** 42% vs 24% was only marginal statistically (p between .05 and .10). Say "only the real reason moved the number".
- **That a real reason "worked" for the big request.** It still got under half (42%). Don't say "most people said yes".
- **Any statistic for raises, salaries, landlords or negotiations** ("adding 'because' gets you X% more"). Not researched: the scenes are fiction.
- **"Harvard study, 1977"** (a popular-blog claim). The paper was published in **1978**; Experiment 1 ran at the **CUNY Graduate Center** copier. Langer's byline was Harvard, so "a 1978 study led by Harvard psychologist Ellen Langer" is fine, but don't place the copier at Harvard. The run date wasn't checked.
- **"Langer herself asked people to cut in."** The paper thanks Cynthia Weinman for conducting Experiment 1, and describes a female and a male experimenter. Say "researchers" or "a researcher".
- **Exact Folkes 1985 numbers, or "the study was debunked".** Neither was read directly. At most: "later work found people do weigh the reason, especially when it matters".
- **"Because" as a magic word that always works / "guaranteed".** The effect is from one 1978 field study with about 15–25 people per group.
- **Real-person likenesses** of Langer, Cialdini or anyone else, and **real brand logos** (e.g. a "Xerox" logo on the copier). Saying "xerox machine" in the quoted line is fine; the 3D copier stays unbranded.
- **"Free"** in the CTA, and no App Store / URL read-out.
