## Brief
- **Category:** Tactic explainer (mirroring, from *Never Split the Difference*), using a real FBI case plus a dramatized everyday scene.
- **Hook idea (≤ 14 words):** "A robber gave up his secret getaway driver. The negotiator just repeated his words." (14). Alternates: "The FBI's simplest trick is repeating someone's last three words." (10) / "Don't ask 'what do you mean?' Do this instead." (9)
  - Frame-0 visual: a coiled brick phone cord pulls taut. Behind the brick bank, an empty parking spot still has fresh tire marks, and a van's tail-lights shrink down the street. Hook title (works on mute, adds rather than repeats): **"HE NEVER ASKED A QUESTION"** (alt: **"THE ECHO TRICK"**).
- **Story arc (~60–75 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, hook:** the phone cord, the tire marks, the shrinking tail-lights. Spoken line 1.
  2. **3–14 s, the setup:** Brooklyn, 30 September 1993, about 8:30 a.m. Two gunmen try to rob a Chase Manhattan branch, set off the alarm, and hold three employees. Police seal off the blocks. An FBI negotiator, Chris Voss, gets on the phone. The lead robber is calm and controlling, and he lies: he claims there are dangerous partners and says *they* are in charge.
  3. **14–28 s, the mirror:** Voss brings up an unidentified van outside. The robber blurts out something like "you chased my driver away". Voss doesn't know what he means, so he repeats it back as a question: **"We chased your driver away?"** The robber answers that when the driver saw the police, he "cut and run". Voss mirrors again, and more details spill out about a getaway driver nobody had known about. (Voss says the driver was later caught, and he credits the robber's own words.)
  4. **28–33 s, the outcome (fast):** about 12 hours later both men surrender. No shots were fired and no injuries were reported.
  5. **33–45 s, name it:** "That's a **mirror**." Repeat the last 1–3 words (or the critical 1–3 words), as a question. Then stay quiet. The FBI teaches it as one of eight active-listening skills (it calls it "reflection"). Voss calls it the closest thing to "a Jedi mind trick". It works because "What do you mean?" sounds like a challenge, while a mirror sounds like "help me understand", so people reword and *add* to what they said.
  6. **45–60 s, "Imagine…" at work (dramatized):** a boss drops by: "Print two copies of everything." → "I'm sorry… two copies?" → "One for us, one for the client." → "Where should we store ours? The file room's full." → "Anywhere." → "Anywhere?" (four seconds of silence) → "…Actually, just make two digital backups." The tower of paper collapses back into one USB stick.
  7. **60–66 s, quick proof beat (optional):** in a Dutch restaurant study, a waitress who repeated orders back word-for-word got bigger tips than when she just said things like "coming right up".
  8. **66–72 s, takeaway + CTA**, then the 2.4 s end hold.
- **Tactics (1–2):** **Mirroring** (repeat the last/critical 1–3 words with a curious, upward tone), paired with **the effective pause** (silence of at least about 4 seconds so the mirror can work).
- **Takeaway (one line):** "Don't argue. Echo their last words and let the silence do the work." (Alt: "Repeat three words, then wait. People will tell you more.")

## Facts
**The source and the person**
- *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It*, by Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, published by HarperBusiness on **17 May 2016**. [HarperCollins / Apple Books listings]
- Chris Voss is a former FBI hostage negotiator. He was the FBI's chief (lead) international hostage and kidnapping negotiator from **2003 to 2007**, retired from the FBI in 2007, and founded The Black Swan Group. He received hostage-negotiation training at the FBI Academy in 1992. [Wikipedia "Chris Voss" and its cited sources]
- Voss reportedly spent **24 years** in the FBI. [Wikipedia; speaker bios] (Say "former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator". The "24 years" figure appears in bios, so keep it hedged or leave it out.)

**What a mirror is (Voss's definition)**
- Voss defines a mirror as repeating **"exactly the last 3 words … of what someone has just said, or the critical 1–3 words"**. A mirror uses their words exactly, while a paraphrase rewords. He calls it "about as close to a Jedi mind trick as anything in the entire FBI 8 hostage negotiation skill set" and says it "compels them to say more". [Chris Voss, Black Swan Group newsletter, "4 Hostage Negotiation Steps for 'Hacking' Confrontation," 9 Dec 2014; same idea in the book, ch. 2 "Be a Mirror"]
- He adds that "in many cases (but not all) a mirror may be all you need." [Black Swan newsletter, 2014] (So never say it always works.)
- **The confrontation recipe** in the book: (1) use the **late-night FM DJ voice** (calm, slow), (2) start with **"I'm sorry…"**, (3) **mirror**, (4) **silence: at least four seconds** "to let the mirror work its magic", (5) repeat. [*Never Split the Difference*, ch. 2, as quoted in RiseWithDrew's book summary; the 2014 Black Swan newsletter gives the same first steps with "wait for at least 4 seconds", then "paraphrase"]
- In the book, Voss says the mindset behind a mirror is "please help me understand". Asking "What do you mean by that?" tends to provoke defensiveness, while a mirror gets people to reword what they said, often more clearly. [*Never Split the Difference*, ch. 2, via book summaries]

**The FBI connection**
- The FBI's Crisis Negotiation Unit (CNU) endorses **active listening skills** as a core technique. The FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin lists **eight active-listening responses**: I-message, paraphrase, emotion label, summary, effective pause ("silence before or after saying something meaningful"), minimal encourager, **reflection ("repeat of the last few words spoken by the subject")**, and open-ended questions. [Dalfonzo & Deitrick, "Focus on Training: An Evaluation Tool for Crisis Negotiators," *FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin*, 9 Oct 2015]

**The documented case: Chase Manhattan, Brooklyn, 30 Sept 1993**
- About 8:30 a.m., two gunmen trying to rob a Chase Manhattan branch accosted the security guard and triggered an alarm. They held **three employees** (the guard and two female employees) for **nearly 12 hours**. Police sealed off about a dozen blocks, with sharpshooters on rooftops. One gunman surrendered about 5 p.m. The other began releasing the women at 7:30 p.m. and surrendered about 8 p.m. **No shots were fired and there were no injuries.** [AP wire brief, "Hostages seized in attempted robbery," *St. Petersburg (Tampa Bay) Times*, 1 Oct 1993; *Washington Post* archive, "Two would-be robbers take hostages in N.Y. bank," 1 Oct 1993]
- Voss places the bank at Seventh Avenue and Carroll Street, Brooklyn. The FBI negotiation team worked from a command post across the street, with several people listening in on the line. [*Never Split the Difference*, ch. 2, via RiseWithDrew summary]
- Voss writes that the lead robber was "feeding us all kinds of misinformation". He wanted the FBI to think he had co-conspirators from several countries who were more volatile than he was, and he kept saying he wasn't in charge. [*Never Split the Difference*, ch. 2, via RiseWithDrew summary]
- **The mirror moment, in Voss's own words:** investigators had identified the drivers of all the vehicles outside except one van. When Voss brought up the van, the robber said, in effect, **"you chased my driver"**, which made no sense to Voss at the time. So Voss mirrored: **"We chased your driver away?"** The robber replied that **when the driver saw the police, he "cut and run"**. Voss "mirrored him a couple times", and the robber, normally "in control at all times", began "blurting out" details about a **getaway driver investigators hadn't known about**. [Chris Voss, Roadman Cycling podcast ep. 1 transcript; also told in *Never Split the Difference*, ch. 2]
- Voss says, "My guy cut and run when he saw the police." He says they then found the getaway driver, who was taken into custody, and that "we had no evidence of him at all other than the spontaneous admissions" of the robber. In another interview he says those taped admissions are why the driver pleaded guilty. [Chris Voss, BiggerPockets Podcast #260 transcript; Roadman Cycling podcast] (Attribute it: "Voss says the driver was caught.")

**Supporting research on repeating people's words**
- **van Baaren, Holland, Steenaert & van Knippenberg (2003)**, "Mimicry for money: Behavioral consequences of imitation," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 39(4):393–398. In a restaurant in the Netherlands, a waitress either repeated customers' orders back **verbatim** or gave positive acknowledgements ("coming right up"). She got **significantly larger tips** when she mimicked. Experiment 2 added a baseline and again found mimicry led to larger tips. [JESP 2003, DOI 10.1016/S0022-1031(03)00014-3; abstract summaries; Harvard PON "Mirror, Mirror," 2010]
- Harvard's Program on Negotiation (adapting Galinsky, Maddux & Ku) reports that buyers told to mimic sellers' mannerisms in a simulated negotiation reached **a greater percentage of interest-based deals**. Mimicking in a job-negotiation exercise led to **higher joint gains**, and pairs with a mimicker reported more liking, rapport and perspective-taking. [PON, "Mirror, Mirror," 21 Sept 2010; Maddux, Mullen & Galinsky, *JESP* 44(2):461–468, 2008] (That research is about body-language mimicry. Present it only as "mirroring builds rapport", not as proof of verbal mirrors.)
- **Swaab, Maddux & Sinaceur (2011)**, "Early words that work," *JESP* 47(3):616–621. In online negotiations, negotiators who mimicked their counterpart's language in the **first 10 minutes** got better individual outcomes than those who mimicked in the last 10 minutes or not at all, and **trust** explained the effect. [JESP 2011; abstract summaries] (Optional; keep it general.)
- Chartrand & Bargh (1999), "The chameleon effect," *JPSP* 76(6):893–910. People unconsciously mimic one another, and being mimicked "increases liking between interaction partners" and makes interactions smoother. [JPSP 1999 abstract via OpenAlex]

The employee mirrored "two copies?" and later "anywhere?", and the boss ended up settling for digital backups. [Voss, Black Swan newsletter 2014; book ch. 2] The video's dialogue, names and visuals are **illustrative fiction**. Don't present the lines as quotes, and don't call the result typical.

## Do not say

(2003), with **one waitress**, not groups of waiters. I couldn't open the primary paper to check the exact means (secondary sites quote 2.97 vs 1.76 guilders, about 68–70%). Say only "bigger tips". Left out.
- **"67% vs 12.5% of deals"** (Maddux et al. 2008). These figures come from secondary summaries I couldn't check against the paper, and the study is about **nonverbal** mimicry. Left out.
- **Exact verbatim dialogue as a quote card.** Voss's retellings differ slightly ("you chased my driver" / "you guys chased my driver away"). On screen, use the mirror itself ("…chased your driver away?") and attribute it as "Voss recalls".
- **When or how the driver was caught.** Voss says "by the end of the day … in handcuffs" in one interview and that the driver pleaded guilty in another. Neither is independently confirmed. Say only "Voss says the driver was later caught."
- **The robbers' names** (Chris Watts, Bobby Goodwin appear in summaries). Not needed and not independently verified, and the DealCalm rule is no real-person likenesses. Left out.
- **Violent details from the book** (pistol-whipping, the empty-chamber "click"). They're graphic, and the AP brief reports "no injuries", which conflicts. Left out. Show the hostages as safe.
- **"It was Voss's first hostage negotiation"** and **"the first NYC bank hostage standoff in nearly 20 years"**. These come from summaries and Voss's own framing and weren't independently verified. Left out (or say "early in his career", since his training was in 1992).
