## Facts
**Background**
- In 1800, Spain secretly agreed to give Louisiana back to France (the "retrocession"). [EB1911; State Dept]
- Spain's 1795 Pinckney treaty guaranteed Americans the right to navigate the Mississippi and to transfer goods to ocean-going ships at New Orleans (the "right of deposit"). [State Dept; EB1911]
- The Spanish authorities suspended the right of deposit in late 1802 (usually dated October 1802). This alarmed Americans and led to warlike votes in Congress. [EB1911; secondary summaries] → say "in late 1802".
- Napoleon planned to retake the sugar colony of Saint-Domingue from a slave rebellion and use Louisiana as the granary for his empire. France sent a large army there. [State Dept]
- Jefferson wrote: "There is on the globe one single spot, the possessor of which is our natural and habitual enemy. It is New Orleans." [State Dept]

**The mission**
- Jefferson sent James Monroe to join Robert Livingston, the US minister in France, to try to buy New Orleans and West Florida for as much as $10 million. If that failed, they were to try for a military alliance with England. [State Dept] NARA puts it as "up to $10 million solely for the port of New Orleans and the Floridas". [NARA]
- Monroe came in sight of the French coast on April 7, 1803, and reached his Paris hotel on Tuesday, April 12. [Adams]

**The other side's pressures**
- Yellow fever was destroying the French army in Saint-Domingue, and war between France and England still threatened. Napoleon decided to give up his plans for Louisiana. [State Dept]
- General Charles Leclerc, the expedition's commander and Napoleon's brother-in-law, died of yellow fever on November 2, 1802. [secondary summaries citing NPS/Britannica] → say "Leclerc died of yellow fever in late 1802".
- Napoleon feared Britain would seize Louisiana as its first act of war. [Adams, from Barbé-Marbois's account]
- Napoleon's main justification for the sale was the need to pay for the coming war. [Adams, from Lucien Bonaparte's memoirs]
- Britain declared war on France on May 18, 1803, ending the Peace of Amiens. This was after the treaty had been agreed. [Britannica via search summary]

**The offer and the haggle**
- According to Barbé-Marbois's own later account, Napoleon told him on April 11: "Irresolution and deliberation are no longer in season; I renounce Louisiana. It is not only New Orleans that I cede; it is the whole colony, without reserve." [Adams quoting Barbé-Marbois's *History of Louisiana*] → hedge: "Barbé-Marbois later wrote that Napoleon told him…". Adams warns that Marbois's Easter-Sunday story was "largely of his own invention".
- On April 11, 1803, Talleyrand (not Marbois) was the first to raise it with Livingston. Livingston wrote to Madison: "M. Talleyrand asked me this day… whether we wished to have the whole of Louisiana. I told him no; that our wishes extended only to New Orleans and the Floridas." Talleyrand said "he would wish to know 'what we would give for the whole.'" Livingston suggested about 20 million francs, provided US citizens' claims were paid. [Adams quoting Livingston to Madison; EB1911; State Dept: "a surprised Monroe and Livingston"]
- Barbé-Marbois then handled the money talks. At a midnight meeting (around April 13), he passed on a price of 100 million francs, with the US also paying its own citizens' claims. Then he "dropped at once from one hundred millions to sixty, with estimated claims to the amount of twenty millions more". Livingston kept insisting the US only wanted New Orleans and the Floridas, and protested the price. [Adams quoting Livingston to Madison]
- Livingston wrote that night: "The field open to us is infinitely larger than our instructions contemplated." [Adams quoting Livingston to Madison]
- On April 29, the Americans offered 50 million francs to France plus 20 million for the claims, 70 million in total. Marbois would only go ahead at 80 million, and "then at last the American commissioners gave way." [Adams quoting Monroe's memoranda]
- Final terms: 60 million francs ($11,250,000) to France, plus up to 20 million francs ($3,750,000) for American citizens' claims against France, for $15 million in total. [NARA transcript note; Adams]
- The Americans feared Napoleon might withdraw the offer at any time. [NARA: "As Napoleon threatened to take back the offer…"; Wikipedia citing Malone] → say "they worried the offer could vanish".
- Taking the whole territory "far exceeded their instructions from President Jefferson, [but] they agreed." [State Dept] EB1911 says they accepted it "without instructions".

**The deal**
- The treaty is dated April 30, 1803, and was signed in Paris by Livingston, Monroe and Barbé-Marbois. According to Livingston, the treaty and the 60-million-franc convention were actually signed on May 2 and back-dated to April 30. [NARA; Adams quoting Livingston] → say "dated April 30, 1803".
- The US got about 828,000 square miles (about 530 million acres) west of the Mississippi for $15 million. This doubled the size of the United States. [NARA; State Dept; LoC]
- NARA says "For roughly 4 cents an acre"; the LoC says "approximately four cents an acre". Dividing $15M by about 530M acres gives under 3 cents; see "Do not say". [NARA; LoC]
- The territory became all or part of 15 modern US states. [State Dept]
- NARA: an area larger than Great Britain, France, Germany, Italy, Spain and Portugal combined. [NARA]
- The treaty never defined the boundaries. When Livingston asked about them, Talleyrand reportedly said: "You have made a noble bargain for yourselves, and I suppose you will make the most of it." [Adams, quoting the envoys' reports] → hedge: "reportedly".
- According to Barbé-Marbois, Livingston said after the signing: "We have lived long, but this is the noblest work of our whole lives… From this day the United States take their place among the powers of the first rank." [Wikipedia citing LPB; Barbé-Marbois's memoir] → hedge: "Livingston reportedly said".
- Jefferson read the Constitution strictly and found nothing in it that let him buy territory, but he went ahead without an amendment. [State Dept; NARA]
- The Senate ratified the treaty on October 20, 1803 [LoC], by a vote of 24 to 7 [senate.gov via search summary].
- France formally handed Lower Louisiana to the US in New Orleans on December 20, 1803. Laussat signed for France; Claiborne and Wilkinson received it for the US. [NPS / Louisiana State Museum via search summary]
- The banks Baring (London) and Hope & Co. (Amsterdam) bought the US 6% bonds and advanced France the cash. [secondary summaries; Adams: "sixty million francs, in the form of United States six-per-cent bonds"] → optional; don't give exact discount figures.
- "Thousands of American Indians across dozens of tribes" lived on the land, and the purchase ignored them. [NARA] → the US bought France's claim to the territory, not the land from the people living there.

## Do not say (UNSURE or disputed; left out)
- **"Half a continent"** as a literal claim. 828,000 sq mi is about 27% of today's lower 48 states and under 10% of North America. Use "double the country" (NARA, LoC) or "a territory bigger than six European countries combined" (NARA).
- **"Barbé-Marbois offered them all of Louisiana"** as the first offer. Talleyrand first asked Livingston on April 11; Marbois then negotiated the price. Say "France offered them all of Louisiana".
- **"They said yes on the spot."** They haggled for about two and a half weeks: over 100M francs asked, 70M offered, 80M agreed. Say "they bargained, then said yes".
- **"4 cents an acre" as exact math.** NARA and the LoC say "roughly" or "approximately" four cents, but straight division gives about 2.8 cents (Wikipedia: "less than three cents per acre"). Safest spoken versions: "pennies an acre" or "about three to four cents an acre".
- **"Monroe and Livingston bought the land from its owners."** They bought France's claim, and Native nations lived there. Avoid "empty land" or "uninhabited".
- **The exact French death toll in Saint-Domingue.** Figures vary widely (some summaries say "about 50,000"). Say "yellow fever wrecked the army".
- **Napoleon's "I renounce Louisiana" and Livingston's "noblest work" as word-for-word facts.** Both come from Barbé-Marbois's memoir, written decades later, and Henry Adams says Marbois's Easter-Sunday story was largely made up. Use them only with a hedge ("he later wrote…", "reportedly").
- **That the $10 million budget was for New Orleans alone.** The State Dept says New Orleans and West Florida; NARA says New Orleans and the Floridas. "To buy New Orleans" is fine as shorthand; "New Orleans and a slice of Florida" is exact.
- **Pierre du Pont de Nemours's back-channel role.** Only Wikipedia has it, so leave it out.
- **Jefferson being angry with the envoys,** or any quote of his reaction. Not verified.
- **Modern-dollar equivalents.** They vary by method; leave them out.
- **Superlatives** ("the greatest real estate deal ever"). The LoC says it "is considered the greatest real estate deal in history"; if you use it, say "often called".
- **The exact date and the official who suspended the right of deposit** (Intendant Juan Ventura Morales, October 1802). Only secondary summaries give this; say "in late 1802".
- **The exact bank discount or instalment terms** of the Baring–Hope bond deal. Not verified from a primary source.
