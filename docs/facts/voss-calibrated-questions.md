## Facts

**The source and the person**
- *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It* by Chris Voss with Tahl Raz was first published in the US by Harper Business in 2016 (UK: Random House Business, 2016). [Penguin Random House UK official sample, copyright page]
- Voss writes that he spent more than two decades in the FBI, fifteen years of it negotiating hostage situations, and that he was the Bureau's one **lead international kidnapping negotiator**. [*NSTD* ch. 1, official sample] His company says he held that role "prior to 2008" during a 24-year FBI career, and that he trained in negotiation with the FBI, Scotland Yard and Harvard Law School. [Black Swan Group, "Chris Voss" bio page]

**What a calibrated question is (Voss's definitions)**
- Voss calls the tactic **calibrated questions**: "queries that the other side can respond to but that have no fixed answers." He says "it buys you time" and "gives your counterpart the illusion of control—they are the one with the answers and power after all." [*NSTD* ch. 1, official sample]
- Ch. 7 is titled "Create the Illusion of Control". Voss writes that calibrated questions avoid "can," "is," "are," "do," or "does" (yes/no openers) and that it's "best to start with 'what,' 'how,' and sometimes 'why'". "Why" can backfire. His summary rule: "Don't ask questions that start with 'Why' unless you want your counterpart to defend a goal that serves you. 'Why' is always an accusation, in any language." [*NSTD* ch. 7, via Kindle highlights (The Modern Spartan book notes); TOC in official sample]
- Voss's standby list: "What about this is important to you?", "How can I help to make this better for us?", "How would you like me to proceed?", "What is it that brought us into this situation?", "How can we solve this problem?", "What's the objective? / What are we trying to accomplish here?", and "How am I supposed to do that?" [*NSTD* ch. 7, Kindle highlights]
- He calls "How am I supposed to do that?" "one of the greatest-of-all-time calibrated questions" and says "you really are asking for help and your delivery must convey that." He also writes: "'how' engages because 'how' asks for help." [*NSTD* ch. 7, Kindle highlights]
- He calls it "the first and most common 'No' question". He warns that tone is critical because it "can be delivered as either an accusation or a request for assistance," and he names the effect **"forced empathy"**: it makes the other side "take a good look at your situation." [*NSTD* ch. 8 "Guarantee Execution", Kindle highlights]
- He says "How" questions are "gentle and graceful ways to say 'No' and guide your counterpart to develop a better solution—your solution." [*NSTD* ch. 8, Kindle highlights]
- Voss's 2025 newsletter: the question "forces the other party to engage with reality and collaborate on solutions". It "shifts the problem-solving burden back to them", and the tone should be "calm, measured, and genuinely inquisitive… not about being passive-aggressive or sarcastic." His example demand: "I need this done by tomorrow morning, no exceptions." [Chris Voss, "The Power of 'How Am I Supposed to Do That?'", Black Swan Group newsletter, 18 Feb 2025]
- The Black Swan Group says the best calibrated questions "often begin with 'What' or 'How'" and reduce defensiveness, unlike "Why did you do that?" [Black Swan Group newsletter, "Taking the Mystery Out of Calibrated Questions"]

**The documented case: Haiti, around 2004 (Voss's account, *NSTD* ch. 6 "Bend Their Reality")**
- As Voss tells it, on a Monday morning in Port-au-Prince the nephew of a prominent Haitian political figure called the FBI. Kidnappers had taken his aunt and demanded **$150,000** ("Give us the money or your aunt is going to die"). Voss sets the scene in the chaos after the rebellion that toppled President Jean-Bertrand Aristide. [*NSTD* ch. 6, quoted in RiseWithDrew, "Negotiation 101: Ask open-ended questions," 28 Jun 2023; Michelle Cheng book summary] (Year "2004" per RiseWithDrew's retelling: hedge it as "around 2004".)
- Context (independent): a kidnapping wave hit Port-au-Prince in 2005, after Aristide's 2004 ouster. [*Los Angeles Times*, Carol J. Williams, "Wave of Kidnappings Heightens Sense of Terror in Haitian Capital," 29 Jun 2005]
- Voss writes that by listening to kidnappers and debriefing freed hostages, his team concluded these crimes "weren't politically motivated at all". The crews were "garden-variety thugs who wanted to get paid by Friday so they could party through the weekend." [*NSTD* ch. 6, via RiseWithDrew] (Attribute it as Voss's conclusion.)
- The family told Voss it couldn't pay $150,000 but could pay **$50,000–$85,000**. Voss writes he "was aiming much lower: $5,000." [*NSTD* ch. 6, verbatim excerpt in Jitha.me book notes and RiseWithDrew]
- Voss advised the nephew to anchor the idea that he didn't have the money "without saying 'No'". **"How am I supposed to do that?" he asked in the next call.** The kidnapper threatened again, and the nephew asked: **"I'm sorry, but how are we supposed to pay if you're going to hurt her?"** [*NSTD* ch. 6, verbatim in Jitha.me, RiseWithDrew and Kindle highlights]
- "Notice that to this point the nephew hadn't named a price. This game of attrition finally pushed the kidnappers to name a number first. Without prodding, they dropped to **$50,000**." The nephew then asked, **"How can I come up with that kind of money?"**, and the kidnapper dropped to **$25,000**. [*NSTD* ch. 6, verbatim in Jitha.me and RiseWithDrew]
- Only then did the family make its first offer, an extreme low anchor of **$3,000**. The kidnapper came down to **$10,000**. The nephew answered with "a strange number": **$4,751**. The kidnapper asked **$7,500**. The cousin then "spontaneously" offered **a new portable CD stereo** and repeated $4,751, and the kidnappers said yes. [*NSTD* ch. 6, verbatim in Jitha.me and RiseWithDrew]
- The aunt was released safely. [RiseWithDrew ("the aunt came home safely"); Michelle Cheng summary ("secured her safe return for $4,751 plus a portable CD stereo before the weekend"); MarketingExperiments, 25 Feb 2020]
- **Honest framing:** the calibrated questions (plus stalling) moved the demand from $150,000 to $25,000 **before the family named any number**. The last stretch to $4,751 used other moves (a low anchor, an odd exact number, a non-cash extra) that Voss teaches in later chapters.

**The Harvard role-play (optional beat; *NSTD* ch. 1, official sample)**
- Voss writes that he came to Harvard for a short executive negotiating course. Robert Mnookin, director of the Harvard Negotiation Research Project, invited him for coffee, and Gabriella Blum joined them. A tape recorder went on the table, and Mnookin said: "We've got your son, Voss. Give us one million dollars or he dies… I'm the kidnapper. What are you going to do?" [*NSTD* ch. 1, official sample]
- Voss replied, "How am I supposed to do that?", then "I'm sorry, Robert, how do I know he's even alive?" To every threat and demand he kept asking how he was supposed to pay and how he would know his son was alive. Blum took over and "all she got were my same questions." Voss ended it ("Okay, okay, Bob. That's all"), and Mnookin said, **"Fine. I suppose the FBI might have something to teach us."** [*NSTD* ch. 1, official sample]
- Timing: Voss says that "a year later, in 2006," he took Harvard Law School's Winter Negotiation Course, so the role-play was about 2005. There he answered a student's offers with "some variation of 'How am I supposed to do that?'", and the instructor (Sheila Heen) told him, "You got literally every dime he had." [*NSTD* ch. 1, official sample]
- Mnookin chaired Harvard's Program on Negotiation for 25 years and directed the Harvard Negotiation Research Project. [Harvard Law School faculty page] Blum joined the Harvard faculty in fall 2005. Before that she spent seven years as a senior legal adviser in the IDF's International Law Department and a year as a strategy adviser to Israel's National Security Council. [HLS PILAC bio]

**Supporting research on asking questions**
- **Rackham & Carlisle (1978)**, "The Effective Negotiator—Part I: The Behaviour of Successful Negotiators," *Journal of European Industrial Training* 2(6), DOI 10.1108/eb002297 (peer-reviewed; ERIC EJ215552). They observed real union, management and contract negotiations. The 49 "skilled" negotiators (rated effective by both sides, with a track record and few implementation failures) were watched over 103 sessions. **Seeking information** made up **21.3%** of skilled negotiators' behaviour vs **9.6%** for average negotiators. The negotiators said questions "give control over the discussion" and are "more acceptable alternatives to direct disagreement." [Paper text, Table VIII; Crossref/ERIC metadata]

## Do not say
- **"It works 8 out of 10 times" / "80% success rate."** This is Voss's own claim in his 2025 newsletter, not research. Left out.
- **"We never had it backfire."** This is Voss's absolute claim about his FBI cases (*NSTD* ch. 8, tied to the separate "José" Ecuador case, which I didn't check). Left out.
- **"The question alone got the ransom from $150,000 to $4,751."** False framing. The questions got it to $25,000. The anchor, the odd number and the CD stereo did the rest. Say that, or stop the question beat at $25,000.
- **"Haiti had 8–10 kidnappings a day / the highest kidnapping rate in the world" / "the kidnap capital of the Americas."** These are Voss's figures and framing, not independently verified for 2004. Left out.
- **The aunt's or the politician's identity, the aunt's age ("elderly")**, and any claim that the case was in the news. Unknown or not independently reported.
- **The exact date (Monday, 2004) as hard fact.** Hedge it: "around 2004, as Voss tells it."
- **Mixing up Voss's other Haiti story** (a 12-year-old dual-national boy taken in a carjacking, released on a Saturday, from his podcast interviews). It's a different case. Don't merge them.
- **"Gabriella Blum was an IDF negotiator for eight years."** That's the book's wording. Her Harvard bio says seven years as an IDF legal adviser plus one year at Israel's National Security Council. If you need it, say "a former Israeli government adviser", or leave it out.
- **Violent lines from the role-play or the case** ("cut your son's throat", threats against the aunt) as on-screen quotes. Keep it non-graphic and show the hostage safe.
- **Overstating Rackham & Carlisle:** the study measured *seeking information* in general (1968–78 labour and contract talks), not Voss's calibrated questions specifically. Say "skilled negotiators asked far more questions", not "science proves calibrated questions work".
- **The Mehrabian "7-38-55" rule** (cited elsewhere in the book). It's widely misapplied. Left out.
- **Neuroscience claims** ("it rewires their brain", "it triggers the amygdala"). Not sourced. Left out.
- **Claiming Voss taught at Harvard or "was trained by Harvard professors"** beyond the attended courses. Not needed.
- **Logos and likenesses:** no FBI seal, Harvard crest, book-cover art, or Voss, Mnookin or Blum likenesses. Use neutral minifigs and generic signage (BANK, LAW SCHOOL, OFFICE).
