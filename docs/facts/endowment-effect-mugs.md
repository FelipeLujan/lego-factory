- **Story arc (~60–75 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, hook:** the mug lands in one student's hands, his price tag flips up, and the neighbour's bid stays low.
  2. **3–16 s, the 1990 experiment:** Kahneman, Knetsch & Thaler hand university coffee mugs to a randomly chosen half of a class. The other half get nothing. Owners can sell, and non-owners can buy. Theory says about half the mugs should trade. **Far fewer than half did.** Owners' median asking price was about twice what buyers would pay. Optional on-screen numbers: about $5.25 asked vs about $2.25–$2.75 offered. Name it: **"That's the endowment effect."**
  3. **16–24 s, curiosity loop: "Maybe the owners just liked mugs more?"** A swap table, from Knetsch 1989: hand people a mug and almost all keep the mug. Hand them a chocolate bar and almost all keep the chocolate. What you start with wins. The reason is loss aversion: giving something up registers as a *loss*, and losses loom larger than gains.
  4. **24–44 s, the dramatized everyday version:** *"Imagine Sam selling his old couch."* To Sam it's movie nights and the dent where he always sits, so his price feels fair. The buyer sees a used couch, and her offer feels like an insult to Sam. Flip side: when Sam buys a used car, the seller's price feels insane to *him*. Same bias, both chairs. (Show two brick price tags pulling apart, a gap labelled "NO DEAL".)
  5. **44–54 s, Move one: spot it on your side.** Ask: "If I didn't own this, would I pay this price?" Anchor to outside numbers (comparable listings, an appraisal), not to the memories. Expect the other side to overvalue *their* thing too, so don't take the gap personally (Harvard PON-style advice).
  6. **54–64 s, Move two: let the buyer feel ownership.** Test drives, "sit on it", trials. Research found that simply touching an item raises people's sense of owning it (Peck & Shu 2009), and that longer handling raised what bidders offered (Wolf, Arkes & Muhanna 2008). Sam lets the buyer sink into the couch, and her tag inches up. Mirror for buyers: a "free trial" is designed to make it feel like yours already.
  7. **64–72 s, takeaway + soft CTA,** then the end card (`DealCalm` / `App Store · dealcalm.com`, 2.4 s hold).
- **Tactics (2):**
  1. **Spot the endowment effect on both sides:** run the "would I buy it at this price?" test and use objective comps. Expect the other side's attachment, and don't treat their number as an insult.
  2. **Let the buyer feel ownership** before the price talk: test drive, hands-on, a trial period. As a buyer, notice when a trial is doing that to you.

## Facts
**The 1990 mug experiment**
- Daniel Kahneman, Jack L. Knetsch and Richard H. Thaler, "Experimental Tests of the Endowment Effect and the Coase Theorem," *Journal of Political Economy* 98(6): 1325–1348, December 1990. [JPE 1990; RePEc record]
- The Nobel Committee's description: "a number of decorative mugs were handed out to a group of randomly chosen test subjects, who were then allowed to choose whether they wanted to sell the mug to someone in a second group who had not received a mug… around half the mugs should be sold. However… those who happened to get a mug valued it more highly… and far fewer than half the mugs changed hands." [Nobel Prize in Economic Sciences 2017, popular science background, nobelprize.org]
- Design detail: subjects first traded **induced-value tokens** (cash-value chips), and those markets showed **no** endowment effect. Then half the subjects were given coffee mugs they could sell to the other half, followed by trials with ballpoint pens. For mugs and pens, **"the median reservation selling price (the WTA) was about twice as high as the median buying price (the WTP)"**, and the effect "showed no tendency to decrease with more trials." [Nobel 2017 advanced scientific background, nobelprize.org]
- The mugs were university (Cornell) coffee mugs that **reportedly sold for about $6 at the campus bookstore**. In the Cornell market runs, the median seller reportedly would not sell for less than **about $5.25**, while the median buyer would pay only **about $2.25–$2.75**. [KKT 1990 as reported in multiple summaries of the paper; Nobel coverage] (Say "about"; the safest spoken version is "about twice".)
- A follow-up condition in the same paper added **"choosers"**, who didn't own a mug but chose between a mug and various cash amounts. Median values were reportedly **sellers about $7.12, choosers about $3.12, buyers about $2.87**. Choosers looked like buyers, not sellers, so the gap tracks *giving something up*, not wealth or liking mugs. This session was reportedly run at Simon Fraser University. [KKT 1990 as reported in multiple summaries] (Optional; currency likely Canadian, so don't say "US dollars".)
- Standard theory (the Coase theorem) predicts final allocations don't depend on who starts with the goods when trading is costless. The endowment effect contradicts this. [Nobel 2017 advanced background]

**What it is, and why**
- Thaler coined the term **"endowment effect"** in "Toward a Positive Theory of Consumer Choice," *Journal of Economic Behavior & Organization* 1(1): 39–60 (1980). [JEBO 1980; RePEc]
- Nobel press release (Oct 2017): Thaler "showed how aversion to losses can explain why people value the same item more highly when they own it than when they don't, a phenomenon called the endowment effect." [nobelprize.org press release, 2017] Popular background: "We normally want more money for selling something we own than we are prepared to pay in order to buy exactly the same item." [nobelprize.org]
- Explanation: "Giving up something we already own is experienced as a loss, while acquiring the same thing is experienced as a gain", and losses are felt more strongly than equal gains (loss aversion). [Nobel 2017 popular background]
- Tversky & Kahneman (1992), "Advances in Prospect Theory," *Journal of Risk and Uncertainty* 5(4): 297–323, estimated a median loss-aversion coefficient of **2.25** ("losses loom larger than gains", roughly twice). [JRU 1992] (Optional. Don't claim this number *causes* the 2× mug gap.)
- The endowment effect "can have long-term consequences, such as reducing trade in goods and services and making legal disputes more difficult to resolve." [Nobel 2017 popular background]

**The swap study (curiosity-loop beat)**
- Knetsch (1989), "The Endowment Effect and Evidence of Nonreversible Indifference Curves," *American Economic Review* 79(5). One group was given a coffee mug and offered a costless swap for a chocolate bar; reportedly **about 89% kept the mug**. Another group was given the chocolate and offered the mug; reportedly **about 90% kept the chocolate**. With a free choice, the split was reportedly about even (≈56% chose the mug). [AER 1989, as reported in multiple summaries] (Safest spoken version: "almost everyone kept whatever they were handed.")

**How strong / when it fades**
- A meta-analysis (Tunçel & Hammitt 2014) of 337 estimates from 76 studies found a geometric-mean WTA/WTP ratio of 3.28. The ratio was **lowest for goods with well-known money value** and highest for public/non-market goods. [Nobel 2017 advanced background] (Optional; don't use it as the hook number.)
- List (2004) found the effect among non-dealers at a sports-card market but **not among professional dealers** trading mugs and candy bars. Professionals who expect to trade goods seem less attached. [Nobel 2017 advanced background]
- Plott & Zeiler (2005, *AER*) used heavy training before eliciting values and found **no significant WTA–WTP gap for mugs**. Isoni, Loomes & Sugden (2011) showed the same protocol still produced a gap for lotteries. The size of the effect depends on the setting, and loss aversion remains the leading explanation. [Nobel 2017 advanced background; AER 2005, 2011]

**Ownership through touch (Move two)**
- Peck & Shu (2009), "The Effect of Mere Touch on Perceived Ownership," *Journal of Consumer Research* 36(3). **Merely touching an object increased perceived ownership** of it, even for non-owners; for non-owners, imagining touching it could do the same. Valuation depended on perceived ownership and on whether the touch felt pleasant. [JCR 2009 abstract]
- Wolf, Arkes & Muhanna (2008), "The power of touch: An examination of the effect of duration of physical contact on the valuation of objects," *Judgment and Decision Making* 3(6): 476–482. **Longer physical contact with an item before bidding led to higher bids** in auctions. [JDM 2008 abstract]
- Test drives, "try it at home" and free trials are **applications** of this research (a reasonable inference). No study here measured test drives directly, so present them as "the same idea", not as a proven price lift.

**Negotiation advice (Move one)**
- Harvard Program on Negotiation articles describe sellers overvaluing what they own (homes, businesses, used goods) and recommend countermeasures such as **objective third-party appraisals / market comps** and taking the buyer's perspective ("would I buy this at this price if I didn't own it?"). [pon.harvard.edu, endowment-effect articles; paraphrase, not a direct quote]

**Dramatized example (fiction; frame as "Imagine…")**
- "Imagine Sam selling his old couch…" / "…buying a used car." Sam, the buyer, the seller and every price shown are **illustrative fiction**. Label them clearly or keep them unspecific, and never call them a statistic.

## Do not say
- **"A $5 mug becomes worth $10."** That isn't what the study measured: the mug reportedly retailed for about $6, and the gap was owners' asking price vs non-owners' bids. Use it only as an obvious "Imagine…" illustration, or use the real medians with "about".
- **"Owners always ask exactly double" / "ownership doubles value."** It's "about twice" in these experiments, and it varies (smaller for goods with a clear market price and for professional traders, absent under some protocols, larger in other settings).
- **"Theory predicted 11 trades and only 2–4 happened."** The specific trade counts weren't verified against the paper. Say "far fewer than half the mugs changed hands" (Nobel wording).
- **Linking the 2.25 loss-aversion coefficient directly to the mug ratio** as cause and effect.
- **"Test drives / free trials raise prices by X%"** or any test-drive statistic: none verified. Present only the touch/ownership research.
- **Ariely studies** (Duke basketball tickets, the "IKEA effect"): left out (data-integrity controversies around the author, and not needed).
- **"The endowment effect is universal / the same in every culture."** PON-reported research (Maddux et al.) suggests it varies by culture. Not verified in detail, left out.
- **Thaler's wine-collector anecdote and any Kahneman / Thaler / Knetsch biography** (Nobel years, deaths, quotes beyond the Nobel text): not checked for this sheet, so left out.
- **Any real university logo** (Cornell, SFU) on the brick mugs; and **real-person likenesses** of the researchers.
- **"US dollars" for the chooser-experiment figures** ($7.12 / $3.12 / $2.87): reportedly from a Canadian session, so currency is unverified.
- **Calling the buyer-ownership move a "trick" for pressuring people:** frame it as letting the buyer experience the item honestly, and flag the mirror for buyers.
