     - Voss calls it a **summary**: paraphrase what they said, label what they feel ("It seems like..."), then go quiet.

## Facts

**The book and the author**
- *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It*, by Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, was published in 2016. [Book; Penguin Random House]
- Chapter 5 is titled "Trigger the Two Words That Immediately Transform Any Negotiation". Some editions add the subtitle "How to Gain the Permission to Persuade". The two words are "That's right." [Book; Penguin UK listing; multiple chapter summaries]
- Voss spent 24 years in the FBI. He was the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator from 2003 to 2007, and founded The Black Swan Group after retiring in 2007. [Black Swan Group bio; Wikipedia; speaker bureaus]
  - On screen: "former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator". For 2000, say "then an FBI negotiator".

**The tactic (Voss's view)**
- Voss calls "You're right" the worst answer to hear. People say it to placate you or get you to stop pressing, without owning the conclusion or changing their behaviour. The Black Swan Group calls it the "'you're right' brush-off". [Book Ch. 5, as summarized; Black Swan Group blog]
- "That's right" signals that the other person feels genuinely understood and has reached the conclusion on their own terms. Voss treats it as a breakthrough that opens the door to influence. [Book Ch. 5; Black Swan Group]
- The tool that triggers it is a **summary**: re-state their view (a paraphrase) and name the emotions behind it (labels). The Black Swan Group's formula: label + paraphrase = summary. [Book Ch. 5; Black Swan Group blog]
- A summary is not agreement: you put their perspective into words without conceding it. [Book; Black Swan Group]
- Voss ties this to the FBI's **Behavioral Change Stairway Model**: active listening → empathy → rapport → influence → behavioural change. [Book Ch. 5, as summarized; Vecchi, Van Hasselt & Romano 2005, *Aggression and Violent Behavior*; docs/facts/fbi-negotiation.md]

**The case: Jeffrey Schilling, Abu Sayyaf, 2000-2001 (independently documented)**
- On August 28, 2000, the Abu Sayyaf group took American Jeffrey Schilling, of Oakland, California, hostage on the island of Jolo in the southern Philippines. [US Justice Department; CBS News; Wikipedia]
- The group demanded a $10 million ransom, alongside political demands. [US Justice Department; CBS News; news reports at the time]
- Abu Sabaya was the group's most visible spokesman and commander in this period. [SFGate; news reports; US Justice Department]
- Schilling was held for more than seven months. He was freed on April 12, 2001, on Jolo, during a Philippine military operation. [US State Department; CBS News; Washington Post; LA Times]
  - Accounts differ on how: officials called it a rescue; other accounts say he got away from his captors during the offensive and was then picked up by troops.
  - Safe wording: "In April 2001, Schilling was free."
- Abu Sabaya was reported killed in a clash with Philippine forces at sea on June 21, 2002; his body was reportedly never recovered. [Guardian; CNN; VOA] (Optional; probably not needed.)

**The negotiation (Voss's account only: attribute it)**
- Voss writes that he coached a Filipino police negotiator he calls Benjie, who spoke with Sabaya. [Book Ch. 5, as summarized]
- Per Voss, Sabaya framed the $10 million as "war damages", tied to historical grievances of Muslims in the southern Philippines. [Book Ch. 5, as summarized]
- Per Voss, after months of stalemate he had Benjie stop arguing and instead summarize Sabaya's worldview and grievances back to him. Sabaya answered, "That's right." [Book Ch. 5, as summarized]
- Voss writes (quoted in summaries): "The 'war damages' demand just disappeared... From that point forward Sabaya never mentioned money again." [Book Ch. 5, quoted by secondary summaries]
  - He adds that Sabaya grew weary of the case and let down his guard, that Schilling escaped from the camp, and that Philippine commandos rescued him.
  - Use it as "Voss writes...", and do not claim it caused the release.

**Optional second example (Voss's own family story, Ch. 5)**
- Voss writes that his son Brandon, a high-school linebacker, kept crashing into blockers instead of slipping past them, and answered coaching with "You're right" without changing. [Book Ch. 5, as summarized]
- Voss then labelled the belief behind it (roughly: "You seem to think it's cowardly to get out of someone's way"). Brandon said "That's right," and his play changed. [Book Ch. 5, as summarized]
- Only use it as "Voss writes about his own son..."; paraphrase it, don't quote it.

- **That the negotiation details are independently documented.** Benjie, the summary, the pause and "That's right" come only from Voss's book. Always attribute.
- **"Sabaya was silent for nearly a minute"**, or any exact pause length. It appears in summaries but isn't confirmed against the book text. Say "a pause".
- **"Two weeks later Sabaya called Benjie and said..."**: only one secondary summary says this, unverified. Left out.

- **"'That's right' is 10x better than 'yes'"**, or any statistic on how often summaries work. No numbers.
