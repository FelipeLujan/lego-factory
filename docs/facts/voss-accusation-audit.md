# voss-accusation-audit: Say the Worst Thing First (the accusation audit)

Format: docs/DEALCALM-TOFU.md (60–75 s, soft CTA, triple hook, DEALCALM STORIES stinger). Category: **tactic explainer** with a clearly dramatized everyday "Imagine…" example, one attributed book example, and one peer-reviewed study. Every claim below was checked with web search on 2026-11-17. **Caveat:** I found **no documented FBI hostage or kidnapping case** where Voss describes using an accusation audit by name. ## Brief

- **Hook idea (8 words, spoken line 1):** "Want to win a tense talk? Insult yourself first."
  - Mute hook title (adds, doesn't repeat): **AN EX-FBI NEGOTIATOR'S OPENER**
  - Frame-0 visual: a contractor minifig in a half-demolished brick kitchen, facing a homeowner who holds a stack of glowing red "accusation" bricks, ready to throw.
- **Story arc (about 60–75 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, triple hook:** the standoff in the kitchen, the hook line, the title.
  2. **3–12 s, the source:** Chris Voss, a former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator, calls it the **accusation audit** (book: *Never Split the Difference*, 2016, with Tahl Raz).
  3. **12–22 s, the method:** before a hard talk, **write down every negative thing they might think about you**. Then say it first, out loud ("You're probably going to think…"). Then **stop talking** and let them answer.
  4. **22–32 s, "Imagine…" (dramatized), the wrong way:** a contractor redoing a kitchen finds a rotten beam, so the price has to go up. He blurts out the new number, and the homeowner fires the red bricks: LOWBALLER! SCAM! LIAR! Freeze, then rewind.
  5. **32–46 s, the right way (dramatized):** "You're probably going to think I lowballed you to get the job. That I'm inventing problems. That you can't trust my numbers." Pause. Each red brick he names drops out of her hands. She says, "…I wasn't going to say that. Show me the beam." (Fiction; no numbers.)
  6. **46–56 s, why it works:** in a 1993 mock-jury study, damaging facts hurt **less** when the defence revealed them first than when the other side did, and the people who disclosed first seemed more credible. Researchers call it **"stealing thunder"**. Voss writes that defence lawyers do the same thing in opening statements.
  7. **56–64 s, real-world nod (optional, attributed):** Voss writes about a student, Anna, who had to cut a subcontractor's share of a government contract. She opened with: "It sounds like you think we are the big, bad prime contractor trying to push out the small business." The reply: "No, no, we don't think that."
  8. **64–72 s, takeaway + soft CTA,** with a callback: the last red brick crumbles to studs, and the finished kitchen snaps together.
- **Tactics (1–2):** (1) **The accusation audit**: list their worst thoughts about you, say them first, then go silent. (2) **Labels** as the delivery ("It sounds like… / You're probably thinking…"). It isn't a confession: you name what they *might* fear, you don't admit to things that aren't true.
- **One-line takeaway:** "Say the worst thing first, and it loses its sting."

## Facts
**Source and author**
- *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It* by Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, published by Harper Business (HarperCollins) on 17 May 2016. [Publisher listings via Apple Books / Kobo / Goodreads]
- Chris Voss is a former FBI hostage negotiator who became the Bureau's **lead international kidnapping negotiator**. Bios say he served **24 years** in the FBI (hedge: "according to his bio"). [Book jacket bio / Goodreads; Black Swan Group "About Chris Voss"]
- Voss's company, The Black Swan Group, teaches the technique and lists **Accusation Audit®** as a trademark. It calls it "a technique we created". [Black Swan Group, "What Is the Black Swan Accusation Audit®?", Derek Gaunt, 27 Dec 2022]

**What the accusation audit is**
- The accusation audit is in Chapter 3 of the book ("Don't Feel Their Pain, Label It"): **list every terrible thing your counterpart could say or think about you, and say it first**, phrased as labels. [Book ch. 3, as quoted in book notes by bagerbach.com and durmonski.com]
- Voss writes that the instinct is to deny a negative ("I don't want this to sound harsh…"), but denying it gives it credibility. Naming it takes its power away. [Book ch. 3 via bagerbach.com notes]
- Voss compares it to defence lawyers who lay out the weaknesses of their own case in the opening statement, which he says lawyers call **"taking the sting out."** [Book ch. 3 via bagerbach.com notes]
- Black Swan defines it as a preemptive tactic: "you speculate what the other side might be thinking and proactively address those thoughts before your counterpart has a chance to voice them." The negatives don't have to be true or fair: "The attempt to verbalize your understanding of their position is more important than accuracy." [Black Swan Group, Gaunt, 2022]
- Black Swan's own example (calling a cable company to lower a bill): "You're going to think that I'm another cheap, irate customer who's going to berate you just to take some aggression out." [Black Swan Group, Gaunt, 2022] (Usable as a second quick "for example" if needed.)
- **It is not a confession.** Black Swan says you're naming issues the other person *might imagine*, "not because they're true but because they could be a barrier." Counterparts often reply "No, that's not it" or clarify their real concern. [Black Swan Group, "Why Accusation Audits® are not confessions," Troy Smith, 18 Nov 2024]
- Use it at the start of a conversation, before delivering bad news, and before making an ask. [Black Swan Group, "3 Places You Need to Be Using an Accusation Audit®," Marcella Oakley, 11 Dec 2023]

**The book's worked example (attribute: "Voss writes…")**
- Voss describes a student, **Anna**, whose firm (a prime contractor on a government contract) had to cut the work and pay of a subcontractor that felt it had been promised the job. The relationship had turned bitter. Anna and her partner, Mark, listed every accusation the subcontractor might make before the meeting and opened by naming them. [Book ch. 3, via durmonski.com and multiple chapter summaries]
- Book quote: "It sounds like you think we are the big, bad prime contractor trying to push out the small business," Anna said, "heading off the accusation before it could be made." The subcontractor's representative replied: "No, no, we don't think that." Voss writes that with the worst accusations laid bare, they were able to turn the conversation to signing the contract. [Book ch. 3, quoted at durmonski.com]

**The research behind "say it first"**
- **Williams, Bourgeois & Croyle (1993),** "The effects of stealing thunder in criminal and civil trials," *Law and Human Behavior* 17(6):597–609 (DOI 10.1007/BF01044684). "Stealing thunder" = revealing negative information about yourself (or your client) before someone else does. In two simulated trials (257 and 148 college students), **stealing thunder significantly reduced the impact of the negative information**, and a path analysis suggested this was because the source seemed **more credible**. [Abstract via Crossref/OpenAlex]
  - Say "in a mock-jury study", not "in real trials". No percentages.

**The everyday example (dramatized; frame it as "Imagine…")**
- The contractor and rotten-beam scene is **illustrative fiction** built on the method above. Don't present its lines as quotes, don't name people, and don't claim a result rate. The homeowner's softer reply mirrors the typical response Black Swan describes ("No, that's not it"), but it is not guaranteed.

## Do not say
- **"Voss used this to free hostages" / an FBI standoff shown as real.** I found no documented FBI case where Voss describes an accusation audit by name. Don't stage one as fact. Left out.
- **"Labeling negatives has three times more impact than labeling positives."** This is a Black Swan blog claim (Oakley, 2023) with no cited study. Left out.
- **Any success rate** ("works 90% of the time", "doubles your chances"). There is no such data. Left out.
- **Extra Anna details from summaries:** "5.5 people cut to 3", "saved a million-dollar relationship", "the government agency cut the budget" vs. "profits turned to losses", and **who said the "big, bad prime contractor" line** (one summary credits Mark, the quoting summary credits Anna). These are inconsistent across secondary sources, and I couldn't check them against the book text. On screen, use only the quoted line and the "No, no, we don't think that" reply, attributed to "Voss writes". Don't name the counterpart (summaries say "Angela").
- **"Voss invented this."** Black Swan calls it "a technique we created", but the idea overlaps with lawyers' long-standing "stealing thunder" and "taking the sting out". Say "Voss calls it the accusation audit."
- **Brain-science claims** ("it shuts down the amygdala", "amygdala hijack"). These come from summaries, not from evidence tied to the accusation audit. Left out.
- **"It always works" / "they'll always say 'no, no'"**: overclaims. Present the softer reply as what *can* happen in the dramatization.
- **Stealing-thunder overreach:** don't claim it works in real courtrooms or in every setting. The study was simulated trials with college students. Later crisis-PR studies exist (e.g., Arpan & Roskos-Ewoldsen, 2005, *Public Relations Review*), but I couldn't read their findings, so they're left out.
- **The 24-year FBI figure stated as hard fact:** it's from bios. Hedge it or drop it ("former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator" is enough).
- **Telling viewers to admit things that aren't true or to fake guilt.** Frame it as naming what they might *think*, not confessing.
- **Logos and likenesses:** no FBI seal, no Black Swan logo, no book-cover art, no Voss likeness. Use neutral minifigs and generic props.
