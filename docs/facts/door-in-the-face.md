# door-in-the-face: Get a No First — why a rejected big ask makes the real ask land

## Brief
- **Category:** Tactic explainer + dramatized everyday scenario (asking for a raise or quoting a freelance rate), plus how to spot it when it's used on you.
- **Hook idea (≤ 14 words):** "Want a yes? Get a no first." (7 words). Alternates: "Same favor, same people. Asking for more first tripled the yeses." (11) / "Scientists got a door slammed in their face. On purpose." (10)
  - Frame-0 visual: a brick front door SLAMS on a minifig with a clipboard, then instantly swings back open as he holds up a much smaller sign. Hook title (works on mute, adds rather than repeats): **"SAME FAVOR. 3× THE YESES."** (50% vs 16.7% is a 3× ratio.)
- **Story arc (~60–75 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, hook:** door slam → reopen. "Want a yes? Get a no first."
  2. **3–15 s, the 1975 experiment:** on a university campus, a researcher asks students to be an unpaid counselor for juvenile delinquents, 2 hours a week, for at least 2 years (a brick calendar wall of ~104 weekly boxes stacks up). They say no. He retreats: "Then could you chaperone them on a 2-hour trip to the zoo?" Brick scoreboard: asked straight, **about 17%** said yes. After turning down the big ask, **50%** said yes.
  3. **15–25 s, curiosity loop: "Is it just that the second ask looks small?"** Not quite. Shown both options at once, only **25%** agreed. And when a *different* person made the small ask after the refusal, the boost disappeared. Name it: **"That's a reciprocal concession."** They gave ground, so you feel you owe some back.
  4. **25–30 s, fast proof beat:** 45 years later, researchers in Cologne, Germany, re-ran the study with a much bigger sample. Compliance came out similarly high. (Flag-swap on the scoreboard, "1975 → 2020".)
  5. **30–46 s, Move one (dramatized):** *"Imagine Maya, a freelance designer."* She first proposes the full package she'd genuinely deliver (big brick crate: logo + site + monthly retainer). The client says no. She retreats to the project she actually wanted (small crate). The client nods: it feels like meeting halfway. Rule on screen: **the big ask must be real and defensible.** An absurd opener can sour the deal (Harvard PON caution on aggressive opening offers).
  6. **46–60 s, Move two: spot it when it's used on you:** *"Imagine a salesperson: the premium plan, no? OK, just the add-on."* Show the three tells as brick warning lights: (1) a huge first ask, (2) a fast retreat by the same person, (3) you feel a tug to "meet them halfway". The defence, from Cialdini: judge the second offer on its own. If the first ask was a setup, it wasn't a real concession, and you owe nothing back.
  7. **60–70 s, takeaway + soft CTA,** then the end card (`DealCalm` / `App Store · dealcalm.com`, 2.4 s hold).
- **Tactics (2):**
  1. **Rejection-then-retreat (door-in-the-face):** open with a bigger, still-legitimate request, then retreat to your real target, so the retreat reads as a concession the other side wants to repay.
  2. **The defence:** notice big ask → quick retreat → "meet me halfway" pressure, and evaluate the second request on its own merits. A manufactured concession creates no debt.
- **Takeaway (one line):** "A retreat feels like a gift. Check whether anything was really given." (alt: "Their first no might be the plan. Judge the second ask alone.")

## Facts
**The original study**
- Cialdini, R. B., Vincent, J. E., Lewis, S. K., Catalan, J., Wheeler, D., & Darby, B. L. (1975). "Reciprocal concessions procedure for inducing compliance: The door-in-the-face technique." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 31(2):206–215. The subtitle names the technique: following an extreme request that gets rejected with a smaller one increases compliance with the smaller one. [JPSP 1975; citation as listed in Genschow et al. 2021, PubMed 33030935]
- Experiment 1: people approached on a university campus were first asked to serve as an **unpaid counselor for juvenile delinquents (at a county juvenile detention center), about 2 hours a week for a minimum of two years**. After they refused, the requester asked for the target favor: **chaperoning a group of juvenile delinquents on a 2-hour trip to the zoo**. [Cialdini et al. 1975, as summarized in course readings (MIT/Harvard copies) and textbook summaries]
- Results of Experiment 1 (reportedly n = 24 per condition): **16.7% (about 1 in 6, "17%")** agreed when asked only for the zoo trip; **50%** agreed after first turning down the two-year request; **25%** agreed in an "exposure control" where both requests were described up front and people could choose either. [Cialdini et al. 1975, Exp. 1, via multiple summaries] (Say "about 17%" or "1 in 6" vs "half".)
- Experiment 2: when a **different person** made the smaller request after the refusal, the effect didn't appear. The concession had to come from the same requester. [Cialdini et al. 1975, Exp. 2, via summaries]
- Experiment 3: the boost required a real **concession** (moving down from a large request). A first request of **similar size** (e.g., a different 2-hour chaperone trip) did not raise compliance above asking for the zoo trip alone. Reportedly about **54%** in the rejection-then-moderation condition vs about **33%** in each control. [Cialdini et al. 1975, Exp. 3, via summaries] (Optional; the hedged "about" is mandatory.)
- The authors explained it with the **reciprocity norm applied to concessions**: when a requester retreats from a large request, the target feels pressure to reciprocate with a concession of their own, i.e., agree to the smaller request. [Cialdini et al. 1975; Genschow et al. 2021 abstract]

**It replicated**
- Genschow, Westfal, Crusius et al. (2021), "Does social psychology persist over half a century? A direct replication of Cialdini et al.'s (1975) classic door-in-the-face technique," *JPSP* 120(2):e1–e7 (Social Cognition Center Cologne, University of Cologne). The abstract says compliance rates were "similarly high" to those Cialdini et al. found **45 years earlier**. Participants were more likely to comply after turning down an extreme request than those exposed only to the target request or to a similarly small first request. The authors say this supports the role of **reciprocity norms**. [PubMed 33030935 abstract, read directly]
- The replication was reportedly run in Germany with a much larger sample (roughly 400 participants). [Psychology Today / SPSP coverage] (Say "a much bigger sample"; avoid the exact count.)

**Boundary conditions (meta-analyses)**
- Meta-analyses by O'Keefe & Hale (1998, *Communication Yearbook* 21; 2001, *Communication Reports* 14:31–38) reportedly found the door-in-the-face effect is real but **modest on average**, and **stronger when the same person makes both requests, when there's no delay between them, and when the request is prosocial** (e.g., volunteering, charity). [O'Keefe & Hale 1998/2001, via summaries] (Keep it qualitative: "works best when the same person asks right away".)

**Cialdini's *Influence***
- Robert Cialdini's book *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion* calls the technique **"rejection-then-retreat"** and ties it to the reciprocity rule. [Influence; cialdini.com]
- Cialdini opens the topic with a personal anecdote: a Boy Scout asked him to buy **$5 circus tickets**; he said no; the boy then offered **$1 chocolate bars**, and Cialdini bought two, even though he didn't like chocolate bars. [Influence, ch. on reciprocation, as widely quoted] (Optional cold-open alternative; neutral scout-like minifig, no BSA insignia.)
- Cialdini's defence: don't reject every offer or concession; accept genuine ones in good faith, but if a "concession" turns out to be a compliance device, **redefine it as a trick**, and then the reciprocity rule no longer obliges you to respond with a concession. [Influence, "How to say no" section, as summarized]

**Using it in a negotiation (cautions)**
- Harvard PON describes the door-in-the-face as following an extreme request with a more moderate target request, working through reciprocal concessions, and cautions that if the other side sees the extreme request as a ploy it can backfire and damage trust. [pon.harvard.edu, via search summary] (Keep generic.)
- PON articles on first offers warn that an overly aggressive opening offer can alienate the other side or cause an impasse (a caution attributed to Max Bazerman). [pon.harvard.edu; also verified for `docs/facts/anchoring.md`]

Any prices or rates shown are made up; label them as examples or keep them unspecific. Never present a success rate for raises or freelance quotes.

## Do not say
- **Any statistic for raises, salaries or freelance rates** ("this gets you X% more"). None was researched; the scenes are fiction.
- **"It always triples your yeses" / "guaranteed".** The 3× is one 1975 condition (16.7% → 50%); meta-analyses call the average effect modest. Say "in that study".
- **"100% (every student) refused the big request."** Commonly repeated, but not confirmed against the paper's text. Say "they said no".
- **Exact replication percentages (e.g., "51% vs 34%") and the exact sample size.** They come from secondary sites only; the abstract just says "similarly high".
- **Exact numbers for Experiment 2** (different requester): not found. Say only that the boost disappeared.
- **Calling the zoo trip the "2-hours-a-week" request** (a mix-up seen online): the big ask was the 2-year counseling role; the zoo trip was the small, one-off ask.
- **Naming the university/city of the 1975 study or the students' details** beyond "a university campus": not verified.
- **Guilt vs reciprocity as settled science.** Cialdini et al. and Genschow et al. support reciprocal concessions; O'Keefe & Hale reportedly lean toward a guilt-based account. Don't claim the mechanism debate is closed.
- **The Watergate / G. Gordon Liddy budget anecdote** from *Influence*: not verified for this sheet. Left out.
- **That Cialdini "invented" the tactic** or first coined "door-in-the-face": the 1975 paper's title uses the name, but first coinage wasn't checked. Say "named in a 1975 study".
- **Real-person likenesses** of Cialdini, the researchers or any real company/brand: neutral minifigs and generic signage only.
- **"Free"** in the CTA, and no App Store / URL read-out.
