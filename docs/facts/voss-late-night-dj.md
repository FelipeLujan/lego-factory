- **Story arc (~60–75 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, triple hook:** the handset handoff, the ON AIR light and the title, with spoken line 1.
  2. **3–13 s, the setup:** Brooklyn, 30 Sept 1993. Two gunmen try to rob a bank, trip the alarm and hold three employees. Police seal off the area. The lead robber keeps control of the calls by putting the negotiator on hold and claiming he's on speaker (Voss's account). Five hours in, the team is stuck.
  3. **13–24 s, the switch:** the lieutenant in charge asks FBI negotiator Chris Voss to take over. The robber demands: "Hey, what happened to Joe?" Voss answers slowly, his pitch falling at the end: **"Joe's gone. You're talking to me now."** (as Voss recounts it). He offers no explanation and no question. Visual: a pitch line that drops and lands like a brick settling into place.
  4. **24–30 s, the outcome (fast, team credit):** the team slowly takes the calls back. Voss's lieutenant tells him to end every call himself. After nearly 12 hours, all three employees are freed, both men surrender and no shots are fired. Voss calls it "a phenomenal team effort".
  5. **30–44 s, name it:** "That's the **late-night FM DJ voice**." Deep, soft, slow, reassuring, with the pitch **going down at the end**. Voss says a downward inflection says "I've got it covered", while an upward one sounds like a question and invites pushback. Use it selectively, to make a point. His firm's rule of thumb: playful and warm most of the time, the DJ voice for the few lines that are "written in stone".
  6. **44–50 s, quick proof beat (optional, pick one):** (a) in a University of Miami study, listeners picked the **lower-pitched** version of the same voice as the leader they'd vote for, for both men and women; or (b) in a German experiment, listeners who heard a neutral text read in a slightly happy or sad voice **took on that mood**. Tone travels.
  7. **50–64 s, "Imagine…" (dramatized):** a freelancer reads a client contract with a "work-for-hire" clause. Version 1: "Um, we don't really do work-for-hire…?" (pitch rises, and the client pushes harder). Version 2, in the DJ voice: "We don't do work-for-hire." (pitch falls, plain and friendly), then a smile back into the voice: "Let's find what works for both of us." The clause tile slides off the contract.
  8. **64–72 s, takeaway + soft CTA**, then the 2.4 s end hold.
- **Tactics (1–2):** (1) **The late-night FM DJ voice**: calm, slow, low, with a **downward inflection** on the line that isn't up for debate. (2) **Downward vs. upward inflection**: a statement that ends going down signals control, and one that ends going up invites a counter. Pair the DJ voice with the warm "positive/playful" voice so you don't sound like an adversary.

## Facts
**The source and the person**
- *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It*, by Chris Voss with Tahl Raz (a journalist), was published by HarperBusiness in 2016. [HarperCollins publisher page; also verified in `docs/facts/voss-tactical-empathy.md`]
- Chris Voss is a former FBI agent and the FBI's former **lead international kidnapping negotiator**. He founded The Black Swan Group, a negotiation-training firm. [Black Swan Group bio; Wikipedia] (Hedge his years of service, which come from bios: "about 24 years".)

**The tactic, in Voss's words (book, ch. 2)**
- "There are essentially three voice tones available to negotiators: the late-night FM DJ voice, the positive/playful voice, and the direct or assertive voice." [*Never Split the Difference*, ch. 2, Goodreads quote page; RiseWithDrew summary]
- The book's chapter summary reads: "The late-night FM DJ voice: Use selectively to make a point. Inflect your voice downward, keeping it calm and slow. When done properly, you create an aura of authority and trustworthiness without triggering defensiveness." [Book, ch. 2 key lessons, Goodreads quote page]
- Voss describes it as "deep, soft, slow, and reassuring" ("I switched into my Late-Night FM DJ Voice: deep, soft, slow, and reassuring"). [Book, Goodreads quote page]
- On inflection, as quoted by summaries of the book: when you inflect downward, you signal that you've got it covered. Talking slowly and clearly conveys "I'm in control". Inflecting upward invites a response because it brings in uncertainty, so the statement sounds like a question. [Book, ch. 2, as quoted/summarized by RiseWithDrew "Negotiating Lesson #3: Slow. It. Down." (2023) and other book summaries]
- The **positive/playful voice** "should be your default voice": easygoing and good-natured, "relax and smile while you're talking". [Book, ch. 2 key lessons, Goodreads]
- The **direct/assertive voice** should be "used rarely" and "will cause problems and create pushback". Voss writes that using it is "like slapping yourself in the face while you're trying to make progress". [Book, ch. 2, Goodreads; RiseWithDrew]
- "Slow. It. Down. Going too fast is one of the mistakes all negotiators are prone to making." [Book, ch. 2 key lessons, Goodreads]
- Voss says we "can be very direct and to the point as long as we create safety by a tone of voice that says I'm okay, you're okay, let's figure things out." [Book, ch. 2, quoted by RiseWithDrew]
- **Voss's everyday example (book):** the same voice works in a contract negotiation when an item isn't up for discussion: "If I see a work-for-hire clause, for example, I might say, 'We don't do work-for-hire.' Just like that, plain, simple, and friendly. I don't offer up an alternative, because it would beg further discussion." [Book, ch. 2, quoted by RiseWithDrew]
- The book's confrontation recipe starts with the voice: "1. Use the late-night FM DJ voice. 2. Start with 'I'm sorry…' 3. Mirror. 4. Silence. At least four seconds… 5. Repeat." [Book, ch. 2, Goodreads quote page]
- **Black Swan Group (Voss's firm):** the warm, smiling voice "should be your default voice 95% of the time. The other 5% of the time you need the downward inflecting 'late-night FM DJ voice'. This is the voice of complete calm and complete authority. You don't have to talk over people with it… calmly state whatever you have to say that must be written in stone without inflaming the situation." If they push back, "repeat it again, only with a bit of encouragement in your voice so they don't see you as an adversary". It closes: "'Control' doesn't win deals. Collaboration does." [Black Swan Group newsletter, "How to Gain Control in a Negotiation in 4 Steps", blackswanltd.com] (The 95/5 split is the firm's rule of thumb, not a measured statistic. Say "Voss's firm suggests…")

**The documented case: Chase Manhattan, Brooklyn, 30 Sept 1993**
- About 8:30 a.m., two gunmen trying to rob a Chase Manhattan branch triggered an alarm and held **three employees** (a security guard and two female employees) for **nearly 12 hours**. Police sealed off the area, with sharpshooters on rooftops. One gunman surrendered about 5 p.m. The other released the women around 7:30 p.m. and surrendered about 8 p.m. **No shots were fired and there were no injuries.** [AP wire brief, "Hostages seized in attempted robbery," *St. Petersburg (Tampa Bay) Times*, 1 Oct 1993; *Washington Post* archive, "Two would-be robbers take hostages in N.Y. bank," 1 Oct 1993]
- The branch was at Seventh Avenue and Carroll Street, Park Slope, Brooklyn. [Book ch. 2 via summaries; news summaries]
- Voss writes that in 1993 he "took over as the primary negotiator" at the Chase Manhattan bank robbery and hostage-taking. NYPD Lt. Hugh McGowan, the negotiation team leader, gave him clear instructions, including **to make sure he ended every call**. Until then, "the lead bank robber had been confounding us by continually putting our negotiator on hold or claiming he was putting him on speaker… to keep control of the conversations." The team "made tactical communication choices to slowly take the dynamic back". [Chris Voss, "Seducing The Long Term Relationship Into Success," Black Swan Group newsletter, 7 July 2015]
- In the same newsletter Voss credits "a phenomenal team effort between the FBI & NYPD negotiation teams" for getting "everyone out safely", and names the other primary negotiators that day: Joe Pisano and Dominick Misino (NYPD) and Charlie Beaudoin (FBI). [Black Swan Group newsletter, 7 July 2015] (So "Joe" was an NYPD negotiator. Use first name only, or none.)
- In the book, Voss writes that the team was about **five hours** into the negotiation and "in too much of a hurry, driving too hard toward a quick solution", when the lieutenant in charge asked him to take over as lead negotiator. [Book ch. 2, quoted by RiseWithDrew; an audiobook listener's account on FamilyChristian.com also says "5 hours"]
- **The moment:** when Voss came on the line, the robber demanded, "Hey, what happened to Joe?" Voss replied: **"Joe's gone. You're talking to me now."** He delivered it as a plain downward-inflecting statement in the late-night FM DJ voice, and the book ties it directly to the work-for-hire example: a straightforward declaration with no alternative offered. [Book ch. 2, quoted by RiseWithDrew; FamilyChristian.com audiobook account (renders it "Joe's gone. This is Chris. You're talking to me now.")]

**Supporting research (keep it modest)**
- **Lower pitch reads as leadership:** Klofstad, Anderson & Peters (2012), "Sounds like a winner: voice pitch influences perception of leadership capacity in both men and women," *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 279(1738):2698–2704 (University of Miami). Men and women recorded "I urge you to vote for me this November". Each recording was digitally made higher and lower, and listeners chose. "Both men and women select male and female leaders with lower voices." [PubMed 22418254 abstract; DOI 10.1098/rspb.2012.0311]
- **Tone of voice spreads mood:** Neumann & Strack (2000), "'Mood contagion': the automatic transfer of mood between persons," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 79(2):211–223 (Universität Würzburg). Listeners heard an affectively neutral speech read in a slightly sad or happy voice, and "the emotional expression induced a congruent mood state in the listeners". When asked to repeat the speech, they spontaneously imitated the speaker's vocal emotion. [PubMed 10948975 abstract; DOI 10.1037//0022-3514.79.2.211] (It tested happy vs. sad, not calm. Phrase it as "the mood in a voice can rub off on listeners".)
- **Pitch and confidence:** Jiang & Pell (2017), "The sound of confidence and doubt," *Speech Communication* 88:106–126. Summaries report that confident-sounding speech had a **lower mean pitch** than doubtful speech. [Crossref/OpenAlex metadata; secondary summaries of the abstract] (Optional and secondary only. See "Do not say" on speech rate.)

## Do not say
- **"The voice made them surrender" / "Voss single-handedly ended the standoff."** Voss himself credits a team effort (FBI + NYPD, with Lt. McGowan's instructions). Say "the team slowly took the calls back".
- **"Joe panicked / his voice cracked / he was rattled."** This appears only in an unverified secondary summary (MasterClass page summaries). Don't disparage the first negotiator. Left out.
- **Exact quote wording as a quote card beyond "Joe's gone. You're talking to me now."** One audiobook account adds "This is Chris". Attribute it: "as Voss recounts it".
- **That "I switched into my Late-Night FM DJ Voice…" was said at the exact "Joe's gone" moment.** The quote is from the book, but I couldn't confirm which scene it comes from. Use it only as a general description of the voice.
- **"Science proves slow speech signals authority/confidence."** Jiang & Pell (2017) summaries report that *doubtful* speech was **slower with more pauses**. Research supports **lower pitch** reading as leadership/confidence, not slowness. Present "slow" as Voss's advice, not as a research finding.
- **Any neuroscience claims** ("it rewires their brain", "mirror neurons", "neurochemical calm", "triggers oxytocin"). Not verified. Left out.
- **Black Swan's "brains work up to 31% better in a positive frame of mind" and "people are six times more likely to make a deal with someone they like."** Unsourced figures in the newsletter. Left out.
- **The 95%/5% split as research.** It's Voss's firm's rule of thumb. Attribute it or drop it.
- **Effect sizes or percentages** from Klofstad 2012 or Neumann & Strack 2000. I only checked the abstracts.
- **The robbers' names** ("Chris Watts" appears in summaries) and the getaway-driver story (that's the `voss-mirroring` video). Not needed, not independently verified, and the DealCalm rule is no real-person likenesses.
- **Violent details from the book** (pistol-whipping etc.). The AP reports no injuries. Show the hostages safe.
- **"It was Voss's first hostage case" / "first NYC bank hostage standoff in decades."** Unverified framing. Left out.
- **Calling Voss the "lead international kidnapping negotiator" in 1993.** That role came later. Say "FBI negotiator Chris Voss" for 1993, and "former FBI lead international kidnapping negotiator" only as his later title.
- **"Voss invented the DJ voice / calm crisis voice."** He named and popularized it. Calm, slow delivery is long-standing crisis-negotiation practice.
- **The airline-pilot / Chuck Yeager drawl story (Tom Wolfe, *The Right Stuff*).** Tempting as a hook, but not checked for this sheet. Left out.
- **"Always works" / "works on anyone" / "a manipulation hack."** Frame it as calm, clear delivery that lowers tension.
