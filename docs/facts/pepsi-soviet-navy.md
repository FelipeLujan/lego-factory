# pepsi-soviet-navy: The soda company that bought a Soviet fleet

Format: `docs/DEALCALM-TOFU.md` (60–75 s, soft CTA, triple hook, "DEALCALM STORIES" stinger). Researched 2026-11-17 with web search plus the original articles: NYT (Flora Lewis column, May 10 1989, read in full), Foreign Policy (Paul Musgrave, Nov 27 2021, read in full; it cites AP July 1989, the Financial Times and Jane's), LA Times (Michael Parks, Jul 21 1992, read in full), NPR (Mar 10 2014), BBC (Jun 12 2019) and the Lead Stories fact check (May 24 2022). ## Brief
- **Category:** History story (a famous deal story: Cold War barter, 1959 → 1972 → 1989 → 1991).
- **Hook idea (spoken line 1, 13 words):** "For a moment, on paper, a soda company owned a fleet of warships."
  - The requested line ("For a moment, a soda company owned a fleet of warships.", 11 words) also works, because the NYT wrote that PepsiCo "bought" the ships. "On paper" is safer: PepsiCo never took possession of the ships, which went straight to a scrapyard (FP, citing AP). Never say it "sailed", "commanded" or "ran" a navy.
  - Hook title (works on mute and adds to the line): **"17 SUBMARINES. PAID FOR WITH COLA."**
  - Frame-0 visual: a brick harbour at dusk. A row of grey brick submarines, with a cruiser, a frigate and a destroyer behind them. A crane lowers a giant generic red, white and blue cola crate onto the dock (no real logo) while a counter ticks "1 … 17".
- **Story arc (about 66 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, hook:** the harbour, the subs, the crate, the counter.
  2. **3–12 s, setup (1959 → 1972):** Moscow, 1959. Khrushchev is photographed drinking Pepsi with Nixon at the American exhibition. Pepsi executive Donald Kendall turns that photo into a deal: in 1972 Pepsi gets the Soviet cola market.
  3. **12–22 s, the problem:** Pepsi sells cola all over the USSR, but it gets paid in rubles, and rubles are worthless outside the USSR. You can't take them home. As the NYT put it, the problem "is how to get the money out."
  4. **22–32 s, "Move one: if they have no cash, take what they *do* have":** Pepsi takes payment in vodka. It gets the US rights to Stolichnaya and sells it in America. Cola goes east, vodka comes west.
  5. **32–44 s, the twist, 1989:** Pepsi has 21 plants and wants 26 more, but Americans only drink so much vodka. So Kendall looks for other Soviet goods to sell. He comes back with 17 old submarines at about $150,000 each, plus a cruiser, a frigate, a destroyer and new oil tankers.
  6. **44–52 s, "Move two: line up the buyer before you take the goods":** the old warships go straight to a Norwegian scrapyard and are sold on as scrap metal. The new tankers are leased out or sold with a Norwegian partner. Pepsi was the middleman; the point was hard currency. (Optional hedged beat: Kendall reportedly joked to Bush's national security adviser, "We're disarming the Soviet Union faster than you are.")
  7. **52–60 s, payoff:** in 1990 comes an even bigger cola-for-ships deal, planned to run for ten years. Then, in December 1991, the Soviet Union disappears. Kendall: "We had a multibillion-dollar contract with a nonexisting entity." Pepsi splits it into separate deals with Russia, Ukraine and the other new countries. The harbour from the hook comes back, now with cutting sparks flying off the subs.
  8. **60–66 s, takeaway + soft CTA,** then the 2.4 s end-card hold.
- **Tactics (1–2):**
  1. **No cash doesn't mean no deal: ask what else they've got.** When the other side can't pay in your currency, price the deal in something they *can* give (vodka, ships) and that you can turn into value.
  2. **Know who will buy it before you accept it.** Barter only works if the next buyer is already lined up: US vodka drinkers, a Norwegian shipbreaker, and a Norwegian shipping partner who sold and leased the tankers and carried that risk.
- **Takeaway (one line):** "If they can't pay in cash, ask what they *can* pay in."

## Facts
- **1959:** Pepsi was one of the American brands at the American National Exhibition in Moscow, where Nixon and Khrushchev held their televised "Kitchen Debate". Pepsi photographers recorded Nixon and Khrushchev drinking the soda together. [Foreign Policy, Musgrave, Nov 27 2021; NPR, Mar 10 2014]
  - Accounts differ on how it happened. Kendall recounted in 1999 that Nixon guided Khrushchev to the Pepsi booth [FP]. The 1989 NYT column says Kendall rushed over with a cold Pepsi when he saw Khrushchev wiping his brow [NYT, Flora Lewis, May 10 1989]. Show the photo moment, not who steered whom.
  - Khrushchev's verdict was mixed: Time recorded his reaction as "skeptical". [FP]
- **Kendall:** started as a worker in a Pepsi-Cola bottling plant, was running the international division by 1957 and became president and CEO in 1963. [FP] In 1989 the NYT called him "a cheery, white-haired, extravagantly energetic 68-year-old" and "a truly imaginative businessman". [NYT 1989]
- **1972 deal:** Kendall and Soviet officials announced a deal to exchange equal values of Pepsi and Soviet vodka (Stolichnaya and Sovetskaya). Pepsi became the first American soft drink in the USSR, and the deal locked Coca-Cola out of the Soviet market. [FP] The NYT: Kendall got "exclusive rights to the Soviet market in return for exclusive distribution rights for Stolichnaya vodka in the U.S." [NYT 1989]
- **1974:** Pepsi became the first American consumer product produced, marketed and sold in the Soviet Union. [NPR timeline, 2014]
- **Why barter:** the ruble "was not an internationally accepted currency and Moscow's foreign currency holdings were limited", so companies like PepsiCo took payment in goods they could resell. [LA Times, Parks, Jul 21 1992] In 1989 "the ruble was still worthless internationally." [FP] The NYT: "The problem, as in most deals with the Soviets, is how to get the money out." [NYT 1989]
- **Why ships:** "the American vodka market has limits. So later Mr. Kendall began looking for other Soviet products he could sell to remit Pepsi's ruble earnings. Thus the tanker and the castoff fleet." [NYT 1989] FP adds that Stolichnaya's growth prospects were more limited than in 1972, so growing Pepsi's Soviet sales meant finding new sources of revenue. [FP]
- **Size of the business (May 1989):** "Pepsi has 21 plants in the Soviet Union and wants to open 26 more." [NYT 1989]
- **The warships (1989):** "Pepsico recently bought from the Soviets 17 submarines (for a measly $150,000 each), a cruiser, a frigate and a destroyer. They are being resold for scrap." [NYT 1989] FP: "In 1989, PepsiCo Inc. … acquired 17 submarines, a cruiser, a frigate, and a destroyer from the Soviet Union." [FP]
- **What they really were:** "small, old, obsolete, unseaworthy vessels." [FP]
- **Pepsi as middleman:** "PepsiCo never even took possession of the watercraft. As the Associated Press reported in July 1989, PepsiCo was just the middleman for the vessels bound for the scrap heap, which went to a Norwegian ship-breaker." The ships "were immediately turned over to a Norwegian shipyard to be scrapped." [FP, citing AP Jul 1989] BBC: Pepsi bartered its drink for vodka "and even Soviet warships, which it sold on for scrap." [BBC, Szu Ping Chan, Jun 12 2019]
- **Scrap value:** selling the Pepsi vessels for scrap "may have been worth a few million dollars, which brought the Soviet navy much-needed hard currency." [FP] The Soviet navy had begun selling obsolete vessels for scrap in the late 1980s. [FP]
- **The tankers (1989):** PepsiCo also bought new Soviet tankers "to carry oil, not beverages" in a joint venture with the Soviets and a Norwegian company that would lease them out or sell them. [NYT 1989] FP: two 28,000-ton Soviet oil tankers, bought jointly with a Norwegian shipping firm run by a friend of Kendall's. [FP]
- **The bigger ship deal:** in April 1989, according to the Financial Times, PepsiCo joined a deal with a Soviet trade organisation and two Norwegian firms. It was valued at nearly $2.6 billion and covered as many as 85 Soviet-built ships over a decade, though "the reported value of the deal varies by source". The Norwegian partners marketed the ships and carried the risk of selling them. [FP] Pepsi said the deal would double its Soviet soda sales over a decade and let it open Pizza Huts there. [FP]
- **1990:** PepsiCo signed what NPR calls "the largest commercial trade agreement in history with the Soviet Union". [NPR 2014] To pay for the expansion, Pepsi would sell more Russian vodka in the US and sell and lease Soviet-built ships abroad. Pepsi's 1990 estimate: more than $3 billion in retail sales of cola and vodka, and ship sales likely worth at least $300 million. [LA Times 1992] (Contemporary headline: "Pepsi Will Be Bartered for Ships and Vodka in Deal With Soviets", NYT, Apr 9 1990; seen only through secondary summaries.)
- **Collapse (1991–92):** the Soviet Union dissolved, and Gorbachev resigned on Dec 25, 1991. [FP] Kendall, by then retired as chairman, said in 1992: "All of a sudden, the whole thing was in pieces--hundreds of pieces … We had a multibillion-dollar contract with a nonexisting entity--the Soviet Union." [LA Times 1992] The tanker shipyard (Kherson) was now in Ukraine, which wanted the ship revenue, and the mozzarella for Pepsi's Moscow Pizza Huts now came from Lithuania. Pepsi split the arrangement into separate deals for Russia, Ukraine, Belarus and the other republics. [LA Times 1992]
- **Kendall's gibe (keep the hedge):** a 1989 NYT column reports: "Donald M. Kendall chuckles about his gibe at Brent Scowcroft. ''We're disarming the Soviet Union faster than you are,'' the head of Pepsico told President Bush's national security adviser." [NYT 1989] Use it only as "he reportedly joked" or "according to The New York Times" (see Do not say).
- **The research angle (for tactic 1):** Michael Czinkota, who teaches international business at Georgetown, told the BBC: "The starting point for countertrades is always that something is wrong with the traditional system." The BBC adds that most barter deals are struck "when conventional avenues are blocked." [BBC 2019]
- **The myth, debunked:** the "sixth-largest navy" / "sixth most powerful military" claim is false. Lead Stories rated it false (May 24 2022), quoting a UMass Amherst professor: "The statement is false on several details." FP calls it an internet legend that "in any real sense … is false". [Lead Stories 2022; FP]

## Do not say (UNSURE or disputed; left out)
- **"Pepsi had the sixth-largest navy in the world" / "sixth most powerful military".** Internet myth (FP, Lead Stories). Mention it only as a myth-bust ("No, it wasn't the world's sixth-largest navy"), never as a fact. Also skip FP's Jane's aside (17 subs would have tied India for the 7th-largest attack-sub fleet), because it invites the myth.
- **"We're disarming the Soviet Union faster than you are"** as a flat, unhedged quote, or as something said to President Bush. It comes from one 1989 NYT column passing on Kendall's own retelling, and the person he said it to was Brent Scowcroft. Say "he reportedly joked to the president's national security adviser", or skip it.
- **"Pepsi sailed / commanded / operated a navy", "Pepsi's fleet", "Pepsi admiral".** Pepsi never took possession; the ships went to a Norwegian shipbreaker (FP/AP). "Bought" or "owned on paper" is fine.
- **"Khrushchev loved Pepsi" / "Khrushchev drank it and wanted it for the Russian people."** His reaction was recorded as "skeptical" (Time, via FP), and Lead Stories found the meme false on this point too.
- **"The fleet was traded for $3 billion of Pepsi."** That mixes up the 1989 scrap ships with the 1990 ship-and-vodka expansion deal. Keep the $150,000-per-sub figure for the warships. Give the 1990 deal no single dollar figure, or just say "billions": FP says the reported value varies by source ($2.6B from the FT vs. Pepsi's "more than $3 billion" retail estimate).
- **"Sold to a Swedish recycling company."** The sources say Norwegian.
- **Sub types ("diesel", "Whiskey-class"), ship names, or the cruiser's class.** Not verified for these ships; FP mentions Whiskey-class subs only as a market price example.
- **An exact date for the warship deal** (e.g. "April 1989"). What's verified is spring 1989 (the NYT called it "recent" on May 10, 1989). Say "1989".
- **"Kendall handed Khrushchev the Pepsi"** vs **"Nixon steered him to the booth".** The accounts conflict. Show the photo moment only.
- **"The first plant was in Novorossiysk."** Only weak sources found. Say "by 1974" without naming a city.
- **"Vodka sales collapsed because of an Afghanistan / KAL 007 boycott."** Only secondary sources say so; the NYT only says "the American vodka market has limits". Leave the boycott out.
- **"85 ships delivered" or "Pepsi got 10 more ships before the collapse."** Not verified in the primary sources read here. Say the 1990 deal was meant to run for a decade and the USSR collapsed first.
- **The BBC's "tomato paste for Pizza Hut in the 1970s" line.** The timeline is unclear (PepsiCo bought Pizza Hut in 1977), and it's off-topic.
- **Kissinger's "he would sell the country for a contract" and the Nixon law-firm favour (both in FP).** Off-topic, and they would read as a smear in a 60 s cut. Leave them out.
- **No invented dialogue** between Kendall and Soviet officials, no invented reactions (shock, glee), no real Pepsi/Stolichnaya/Soviet-navy insignia in 3D geometry, and no Kendall figure on the CTA set.
