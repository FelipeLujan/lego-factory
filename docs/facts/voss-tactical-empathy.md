**The Harlem standoff (Voss's own account, *Never Split the Difference*, Ch. 3, 'Don't Feel Their Pain, Label It')**
- Voss writes that in **1998** he stood in a narrow hallway outside a **27th-floor** apartment in a Harlem high-rise. Three fugitives, reportedly heavily armed and recently in a shootout with a rival gang, were believed to be inside. An FBI SWAT team was with him. [Book Ch. 3, as summarized by *Inc.*, SuperSummary and other chapter summaries]
- There was **no phone line** into the apartment, so Voss spoke **through the door**. [Book Ch. 3; *Inc.*]
- He used what he calls his **'late-night FM DJ voice'**: deep, slow, calm and reassuring. [Book; *Inc.*; Black Swan Group]
- Book quotes (Voss's own wording, safe to speak as quotes): "It looks like you don't want to come out," I said repeatedly. "It seems like you worry that if you open the door, we'll come in with guns blazing. It looks like you don't want to go back to jail." [Book Ch. 3, quoted in excerpts and summaries]
- He talked for about **six hours** with no response from inside. [Book Ch. 3; *Inc.*]
- Then the door opened and the fugitives came out and **surrendered peacefully**, per Voss. [Book Ch. 3; *Inc.*]
- Asked later why they came out, they reportedly said: "We didn't want to get caught or get shot, but you calmed us down. We finally believed you wouldn't go away, so we just came out." [Book, as quoted by *Inc.*] (Safer as a paraphrase: 'They said he calmed them down.')

**The tactic (from the book)**
- Voss defines **tactical empathy** as "understanding the feelings and mindset of another in the moment and also hearing what is behind those feelings so you increase your influence in all the moments that follow." [Book; Goodreads quote page]
- Tactical empathy is **not agreement or sympathy**: you show you understand their perspective without conceding it. [Book; Black Swan Group]
- A **label** names the other person's feeling or situation out loud. Voss recommends starting with **'It seems like...', 'It sounds like...' or 'It looks like...'**. [Book Ch. 3; Black Swan Group; MasterClass]
- He advises **against starting with 'I'** (e.g., 'I'm hearing that...'), because it puts the focus on you and can make people guarded. [Book Ch. 3, as summarized]
- After a label, **go silent** and let it sink in. The other side usually fills the silence and elaborates. [Book Ch. 3, as summarized; Black Swan Group]
- Related moves (optional; only one tactic fits): labeling negatives up front (the **'accusation audit'**: say the worst things they may think of you before they do), and aiming for **'That's right'** rather than 'You're right'. [Book; Black Swan Group]

**Not just Voss: FBI crisis negotiation**
- **Emotional labeling** is one of the standard active-listening skills in FBI-style crisis negotiation, alongside minimal encouragers, paraphrasing, mirroring, open-ended questions and effective pauses. These underpin the FBI's **Behavioral Change Stairway Model** (active listening, empathy, rapport, influence, behavioral change). [FBI Law Enforcement Bulletin articles on fbi.gov; Vecchi, Van Hasselt & Romano 2005, *Aggression and Violent Behavior*; docs/facts/fbi-negotiation.md]

**The research Voss points to**
- Lieberman, Eisenberger, Crockett, Tom, Pfeifer & Way (2007), 'Putting Feelings Into Words: Affect Labeling Disrupts Amygdala Activity in Response to Affective Stimuli,' *Psychological Science* 18(5):421-428 (UCLA). In fMRI, participants viewed emotional faces. **Labeling the emotion with a word** reduced amygdala response compared with other tasks (e.g., gender labeling or matching), and increased right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex activity. [Psychological Science; PubMed/NIH; UCLA]
- Voss cites UCLA professor Matthew Lieberman's brain-imaging work in Ch. 3 to explain why labels calm emotions. [Book Ch. 3, as summarized]
- Torre & Lieberman (2018), 'Putting Feelings Into Words: Affect Labeling as Implicit Emotion Regulation,' *Emotion Review* 10(2):116-124. This review reports that affect labeling can reduce self-reported distress, physiological arousal and amygdala activity, acting like emotion regulation even without trying. [Emotion Review; Semantic Scholar]
- Wording to use: 'In a UCLA brain-scan study, putting a feeling into words turned down the brain's alarm (the amygdala).'
