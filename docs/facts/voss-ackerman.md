## Brief
- **Category:** Tactic explainer (the Ackerman model, *Never Split the Difference*, Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, 2016). Built on a documented Voss case plus research, with an optional dramatized everyday scene.
- **Hook idea (≤ 14 words):** "He offered $1,829 rent. The agent said: 'You must be an accountant.'" (12). Alternates: "Never offer five thousand. Offer $4,751. A kidnapping negotiator explains why." (10) / "A kidnapping negotiator's haggling formula has four numbers: 65, 85, 95, 100." (12)
  - **Mute hook title** (adds to the line): **65 · 85 · 95 · 100** (alt: **THE FOUR-NUMBER HAGGLE**).
  - **Frame-0 visual:** a giant brick sign "RENEWAL $2,000/MO" over an apartment door. A minifig hand slaps an oddly exact brick tag reading **$1,829** onto it, and the big sign cracks.
- **Story arc (about 60–75 s):**
  1. **0–3 s, triple hook:** the sign, the slap, the line and the title.
  2. **3–9 s, the setup (attribute it):** as Voss tells it, eight months into a $1,850/month lease, one of his Georgetown MBA students was told the renewal would be **$2,000/month for a year** (or $2,100 for ten months).
  3. **9–15 s, the prep:** comparable places were **$1,800–$1,950**, in worse buildings. His **target was $1,830**.
  4. **15–33 s, the climb (one number per beat):**
     - **$1,730.** The agent laughs. The student asks "How do you price lease renewals?" and notes that one empty month costs about $2,000.
     - **$1,790.** "Give me time to think."
     - Five days later the agent offers **$1,950**. The student: "That is generous of you, but how am I supposed to accept it…?"
     - **$1,810.** "Still lower than the market."
     - Pen and paper, fake calculations: **"The maximum I can afford is $1,829."**
     - The agent: **"Wow. $1,829. You seem very precise. You must be an accountant."** Twelve-month lease, done. (He wasn't an accountant.)
  5. **33–50 s, name it: the Ackerman model.** Voss credits Mike Ackerman, "an ex-CIA type" who became a kidnap-for-ransom consultant. A brick staircase builds:
     - Pick a target and open at **65%** of it.
     - Raise to **85%**, then **95%**, then **100%**. The steps shrink (20 → 10 → 5 points), so it feels like they're squeezing you dry.
     - Get them to counter before every raise.
     - Make the last number **precise**: Voss's example is "$37,893 rather than $38,000".
     - On the final offer, **throw in a non-cash item** they probably don't want.
     - **Callback (4 s):** Voss's team once closed a ransom at **$4,751 plus a portable CD stereo**.
  6. **50–57 s, proof beat:** in Columbia experiments, people who got a **precise first offer countered less** and saw the offerer as **better informed** (Mason et al., 2013). The catch: experts distrust random precision unless you give a reason (Loschelder et al., 2016). That's what the pen and paper did.
  7. **57–66 s (optional), Imagine… (dramatized):** you're buying a used motorbike with a $4,000 target. Offer **$2,600 → $3,400 → $3,800 → "$3,987, and I'll pick it up today with my own trailer."** (Fiction, shown as math, not as a result.)
  8. **66–72 s, takeaway + soft CTA**, then the 2.4 s end hold.
- **Tactics:** the **Ackerman model** (65/85/95/100 with shrinking raises and a counter before every raise), closed with **a precise non-round number plus a non-cash throw-in**.
- **Takeaway:** "Plan your last number before your first, and make it look calculated."

## Facts

**The book and the author**
- *Never Split the Difference: Negotiating As If Your Life Depended On It*, by Chris Voss with Tahl Raz, HarperBusiness, May 2016. [Black Swan Group book page; publisher listing via search]
- Before 2008, Voss was the FBI's lead international kidnapping negotiator. He spent 24 years with the Bureau and taught MBA negotiation as an adjunct at USC Marshall and Georgetown McDonough. [Black Swan Group, Chris Voss bio page]

**The Ackerman model (*NSTD* ch. 9)**
- Voss calls it "an offer-counteroffer method, at least on the surface" and "a very effective system for beating the usual lackluster bargaining dynamic, which has the predictable result of meeting in the middle." [*NSTD* ch. 9, verbatim in Manas Saloi book notes]
- The steps, verbatim [*NSTD* ch. 9, via Manas Saloi notes; same wording quoted by Coaching With Wisdom and Jason Varbedian's notes]:
  1. "Set your target price (your goal)"
  2. "Set your first offer at 65 percent of your target price"
  3. "Calculate three raises of decreasing increments (to 85, 95, and 100 percent)"
  4. "Use lots of empathy and different ways of saying 'No' to get the other side to counter before you increase your offer"
  5. "use precise, nonround numbers like, say, $37,893 rather than $38,000. It gives the number credibility and weight"
  6. "On your final number, throw in a nonmonetary item (that they probably don't want) to show you're at your limit"
- Voss attributes it to Mike Ackerman, "an ex-CIA type." [Coaching With Wisdom (Thomas Noble), quoting *NSTD*] Book notes describe the source as ex-CIA kidnap-for-ransom consulting. [Bagerbach notes]
- **Context (independent):** The Ackerman Group's site says Mike Ackerman served 11 years in the CIA's Clandestine Services before resigning in 1975. It says multinationals have used the firm "since 1977", that Chubb has retained it for 48 years for kidnap/ransom and extortion policyholders, and that his "forte is recovering kidnap victims." [ackermangroup.com] (The match to the book's Ackerman is implicit. Say "an ex-CIA kidnapping consultant", not the firm name.)
- Voss's explanation, verbatim [*NSTD* ch. 9, quoted in Coaching With Wisdom]:
  - The system "incorporates the psychological tactics we've discussed – reciprocity, extreme anchors, loss aversion".
  - The 65% opener is "an extreme anchor, a big slap in the face".
  - The raises "decrease by half each time" and convince the counterpart "he's squeezing you to the point of breaking".
  - "you can't buy anything for $2, but you can buy a million things for $1.99."
- Chapter summary: "Remember: 65, 85, 95, 100 percent. Decreasing raises and ending on nonround numbers will get your counterpart to believe that he's squeezing you for all you're worth when you're really getting to the number you want." [*NSTD* ch. 9 key lessons, verbatim in Manas Saloi and Graham Mann notes]
- **Voss's own company teaches it:**
  - The Black Swan Group's official *NSTD* Study Guide, Ackerman Bargaining page:
    - Buyer: "65% → 85% → 95% → 100%". Seller: "135% → 115% → 105% → 100%".
    - "At final offer, add non-monetary item to show that you're at your limit."
    - "Use precise, non-round, odd numbers."
    - It suggests an accusation-audit opener: "I'm embarrassed to tell you my offer."

**The documented case: the rent renewal (Voss's account, *NSTD* ch. 9)** [all verbatim in Manas Saloi notes; SUN summary corroborates]
- "Eight months after a Georgetown MBA student of mine named Mishary signed a rental contract for $1,850/month," the landlord said renewal would be **$2,100/month for ten months or $2,000/month for a year**.
- He found comparables at **$1,800–$1,950/month**, "but none of them were in as good a building," and set his target at **$1,830**.
- He asked: "Even though your building is better in terms of location and services, how am I supposed to pay $200 extra?"
- His first offer was **$1,730** a month for a year. The agent laughed and said it was "way below market price."
- He asked "how do you price lease renewals?" and argued: "One month unrented would be a $2,000 loss."
- His second offer was **$1,790** for 12 months. Voss notes that two offers in a row is normally "verboten".
- "Five days later" the agent offered **$1,950**. Mishary: "That is generous of you, but how am I supposed to accept it when I can move a few blocks away and stay for $1,800?"
- He raised to **$1,810**. The agent said it was still below market.
- He asked for pen and paper, "started doing fake calculations," and said "the maximum I can afford is **$1,829**."
- The agent: "Wow. $1,829… You seem very precise. You must be an accountant. [Mishary was not.] … I think we can make this work for a twelve-month lease."
- Voss credits "decreasing Ackerman offers, nonround numbers, deep research, smart labeling, and saying no without saying 'No'."
- **Arithmetic (mine):** $171/month below the $2,000 one-year ask, or $2,052 over the lease. His raises were +$60, +$20, +$19, and his offers were about 95–100% of target, not 65/85/95.

**The callback: Haiti, around 2004 (Voss's account, *NSTD* ch. 6; see voss-calibrated-questions.md)**
- Kidnappers demanded $150,000. Voss was "aiming much lower: $5,000". The family offered $3,000 and the kidnapper countered $10,000.
- The family answered with "a strange number that seemed to come from deep calculation…: **$4,751**". The kidnapper asked $7,500. [RiseWithDrew, quoting *NSTD*]
- "We had the cousin 'spontaneously' say he'd throw in a **new portable CD stereo** and repeated the $4,751. The kidnappers, who didn't really want the CD stereo felt there was no more money to be had, said yes." The aunt came home safely. [same]
- Honest framing: this shows the closing moves only. $3,000 is 60% of the goal, not an exact 65/85/95 run.

**Research on precise numbers (peer-reviewed)**
- Janiszewski & Uy (2008), *Psychological Science* 19:121–127, DOI 10.1111/j.1467-9280.2008.02057.x: "adjustment away from a numerical anchor is smaller if the anchor is precise than if it is rounded." [Crossref abstract]
- Mason, Lee, Wiley & Ames (2013), *JESP* 49:759–763, DOI 10.1016/j.jesp.2013.02.012 (Columbia): "Recipients of precise opening offers made more conciliatory counter-offers"; the effect "carried through to final settlements"; precise offerers are "assumed to be more informed." [Paper PDF]
- Loschelder, Friese, Schaerer & Galinsky (2016), "The Too-Much-Precision Effect," *Psychological Science* 27:1573–1587, DOI 10.1177/0956797616666074. Across 1,320 experts and amateurs, precision helped amateurs linearly but was inverted-U for experts, who "saw too much precision as reflecting a lack of competence" unless given rationales. [Crossref abstract]
- Scope: these studies test first offers and anchors. Say "precise numbers are stronger anchors", not "science proves the Ackerman model".

**Everyday example:** the motorbike scene is illustrative fiction (a $4,000 target: $2,600 / $3,400 / $3,800 / $3,987 plus a non-cash extra).

## Do not say
- "Mishary used 65/85/95/100": false (about 95–100% of target, two offers in a row).
- "Mishary threw in a non-cash item": he didn't.
- "He cut his rent by $271": that compares to the 10-month price. Use $2,000/yr → $1,829.
- The Haiti "$15k–$75k ransoms / always under $5,000" and the Miami FBI boss's "one dollar" quote: found only in AI summaries and unlicensed copies of the book.
- "The Ackerman guys worked every case with Voss" or "learned at Kroll": unverified.
- "Ruth" or "Bill" Ackerman: AI errors. The name is Mike.
- Naming The Ackerman Group as the book's source: the match is implicit.
- "Ackerman was a CIA hostage negotiator": he was a Clandestine Services officer who later recovered kidnap victims privately.
- "Concession-getters are happier even when they pay more" as a stat: it's Voss's summary of unnamed research. Attribute it or drop it.
- Any success rate for the model: no data exists.
- "$102.36-style decimals on everything": that's a Forbes Councils contributor's tip, and experts may distrust it.
- Haiti identities, exact dates, or graphic threats.
- The Mehrabian 7-38-55 rule.
- The Toyota 4Runner car story: numbers vary across summaries and it isn't an Ackerman example.
- Logos and likenesses (FBI/CIA seals, firm marks, book cover, Voss).
