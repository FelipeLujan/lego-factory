## Facts
**Tversky & Kahneman (1974)**
- Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman published "Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases" in *Science* (vol. 185, no. 4157, pp. 1124–1131, Sept 27, 1974). It describes three heuristics, one of which is "adjustment from an anchor". They write that these heuristics are "highly economical and usually effective, but they lead to systematic and predictable errors." [Science 1974; PubMed 17835457 abstract]
- In the experiment, a number between 0 and 100 was set by spinning a wheel of fortune in front of the participants. They first judged whether the true value was higher or lower than that number, then gave their estimate (e.g., the percentage of African countries in the United Nations). [Tversky & Kahneman 1974, *Science*; summarized by Simply Psychology, The Decision Lab]
- In *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, Kahneman says the wheel was **rigged to stop only on 10 or 65**. He also says the participants were University of Oregon students. [Kahneman, *Thinking, Fast and Slow* (2011)] (Attribute "rigged" to Kahneman's account.)
- **Median** estimates for the UN question were **25%** for the group that saw 10 and **45%** for the group that saw 65. [Tversky & Kahneman 1974] (Say "median" or "typical guess", not "average".)
- "Payoffs for accuracy did not reduce the anchoring effect." [Tversky & Kahneman 1974]
- High-school students got **5 seconds** to estimate 8×7×6×5×4×3×2×1 or 1×2×3×4×5×6×7×8. The median estimates were **2,250** (descending) and **512** (ascending). The correct answer is **40,320** for both. [Tversky & Kahneman 1974; Simply Psychology]
- Mechanism: people start from an initial value and **adjust insufficiently** away from it. [Tversky & Kahneman 1974]

**Experts aren't immune**
- Englich, Mussweiler & Strack (2006), "Playing Dice With Criminal Sentences," *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 32(2):188–200. Experienced legal professionals anchored on a sentencing demand even when it came from an irrelevant source, when they were told it was random, or when **they rolled it themselves with dice**. "Expertise and experience did not reduce this effect." [PSPB 2006; PubMed 16382081 abstract]
- In the dice study (Study 3), the dice were **loaded to total either 3 or 9** in a shoplifting case. Mean sentences were reportedly **about 5 months** (5.28) after a 3 and **about 8 months** (7.81) after a 9. [Englich et al. 2006, as reported in multiple summaries, e.g., ResearchGate copy of the paper] (Say "about 5 vs about 8 months".)
- Northcraft & Neale (1987), "Experts, Amateurs, and Real Estate," *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes*. Students and professional real-estate agents toured a real house with an information packet in which only the **listing price** changed. A higher listing price led both groups to higher value estimates. The agents tended to **deny** the listing price had influenced them. [OBHDP 1987; summaries in negotiation literature]

**First offers in negotiation**
- Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001), "First Offers as Anchors," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 81(4):657–669. Across 3 experiments, **whichever party (buyer or seller) made the first offer obtained a better outcome**, and **first offers were a strong predictor of final settlement prices**. [JPSP 2001; PubMed 11642352 abstract]
- In the same study, the first-offer advantage **was eliminated** when the other side focused on information inconsistent with that first offer. That meant thinking about **the opponent's alternatives** (Exp. 1), **the opponent's reservation price** (Exp. 2) or **one's own target** (Exp. 3). This held face-to-face and over e-mail. [JPSP 2001 abstract]
- Mason, Lee, Wiley & Ames (2013), "Precise Offers Are Potent Anchors," *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 49(4):759–763. **Precise** first offers (e.g., $X,250 rather than a round number) drew **smaller counteroffer adjustments** than round ones. Recipients tended to assume the precise offer-maker was better informed. [JESP 2013; Columbia Business School]
- Harvard Program on Negotiation (PON) articles generally advise making the first offer, especially an ambitious one, in price negotiations. They also note the risk that an overly aggressive offer can alienate the other side or cause an impasse (a caution PON attributes to Max Bazerman). Going first works best when you know the market and the zone of possible agreement. [pon.harvard.edu]
- PON (citing Harvard's Guhan Subramanian) advises that when facing an extreme anchor, you should **not simply counter**, because that treats the anchor as legitimate. Instead, make clear it's outside the realm of reality, then put your own offer on the table backed by objective criteria. [pon.harvard.edu]
- Magee, Galinsky & Gruenfeld (research reported by PON): negotiators with a stronger BATNA (more power) were more likely to make the first offer and gained an advantage from it. [pon.harvard.edu] (Optional; keep it general.)

## Do not say
- **"First offers explain 50–85% of the variance in outcomes."** It appears in PON/secondary write-ups, but I couldn't confirm the exact figure against a primary paper. Left out.
- **Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec's Social Security number auction study** ("Coherent Arbitrariness", 2003). Replications are mixed and the author faces wider data-integrity controversies. Left out.
- **Any specific effect-size percentages** ("anchors move prices by X%") or invented numbers presented as research.
- **"Always make the first offer" / "the first mover always wins."** The research shows a typical advantage that can be cancelled. PON adds caveats (poor information, overly aggressive offers). Say "usually" or "often".
- **The true share of African countries in the UN** (in 1974 or today): not checked, and not needed.
- **Calling the 1974 wheel "rigged" as if the paper says so.** The 1974 paper says it was spun in front of the participants. "Rigged to 10 or 65" comes from Kahneman's *Thinking, Fast and Slow*, so attribute it or just show it.
- **"Average" for the 1974 numbers:** they are **medians**.
- **The judges' nationality or seniority beyond "experienced legal professionals"**, and calling the dice-study sentences "prison" vs "probation": not verified in detail.
- **Nobel Prize / biographical asides about Kahneman or Tversky:** not checked for this sheet, so left out.
